CHAPTER SEVEN SPONTANEITY I: NOT THINKING

The season of a writer takes place in the mind. The writer collects ideas, reflects upon them, organizes them, expresses them in words, publishes them. The reader's experience is also cerebral, solitary, still, if more immediately and easily gratified than the writer. The season of an actor, however, is hemispherically different. The actor is constantly physical. The actor is always in relation with others. The actor gets quick and tangible feedback. Most distinct of all, the actor does not think in the same way as the writer. This is especially true in the NST, where actors do not even need to read.[1]

[1] A century ago Matthew Arnold had no hesitation in asserting that the way to culture was through “reading, writing, thinking." Yet Harvard English professor Reuben Brower can say in our epoch, “Literature of the first order calls for lively reading; we must almost act it out as if we were taking parts in a play." What sense of the limitations of literacy informs Brower's observation of the need for acting, even while he is advocating “better” reading? (Brower & Proirier, 6)

In Playback Theatre and in my teaching workshops, the hallmark of a performer's success can be capsulized by the term *spontaneity*. Spontaneity is deeply associated with action and a definite type of *nonthinking*.

THE SIGNS OF SPONTANEITY

These are the signs of spontaneity:

*Vitality*. Persons who do not hold back and are able to express themselves directly convey a sense of *aliveness*. They move, they laugh, they surprise, they shine. They convey an appealing warmth or magnetism that is evident from the instant they enter the hall. Actors with this kind of energy are valuable above and beyond their acting ability, especially in nonscripted theatre, where actors are usually on stage as themselves  in between dramatic scenes.

*Appropriateness*. The idea of spontaneity as heedless impulse belittles the potential of the concept, which can include a difficult-to-attain notion of “right action” not unlike the Zen master's injunction to “eat while eating, sleep while sleeping." J. L. Moreno, who developed the spontaneity concept as a cornerstone of his therapy, placed a similar emphasis: Spontaneity, he said, is the ability to perform an old task in a new way or a new task adequately.[2] The actor with spontaneity, then, will always have a measure of the situation, will be adequate to any role, and will know when to blend in and when to stand out.


[2] See Fox 1987, 42. Moreno interested himself in the same primitive impulses which fueled the German Expressionist movement, and in fact, edited a journal called *daimon*. He remained steadfastly committed, however, to redressive purposes.

*Intuitiveness*. The spontaneous nonscripted actor, unre- stricted by a playwright's lines, can say whatever comes, often producing word and action as stunningly à propos as it is unexpected. Thus an actor with a trained imagination and with full access to his or her senses has available a wide range of expressive possibilities.

*Readiness for change*. In a nontextual situation, the unexpected is a way of life. To deal with irregularity in a creative way requires an ability to accept each moment as it comes and respond dynamically. Most important of all is to accept the idea of living constantly with the unforeseen.This challenge presents itself in many ways. The classic manifestation for the improvisational actor, perhaps, takes place on the threshold of an interaction with another actor. Can both enter into the moment-without knowing what will happen yet knowing that their experiment is public--with a sense of adventure and zest?

Spontaneity is a kind of ecstasy, an attunement which encapsulates thought. The artist becomes “like a medium--you try to cross over into that part of you that's always there but is only alive when you're playing." It is related to “the power of creating beyond oneself without losing oneself." It is like a “light breath... a presence as power."[3]

[3] The first quotation is from Anais Nin, the second from jazz pianist Dwike Mitchell, the third from Paul Tillich, the fourth from Martin Buber. (Nin, 93; Zinssner, I44; Tillich, 81; Buber, 109)

As a young man I lived for a time on a farm in the West Country of England. Nearby was a cafe which occasionally sponsored poetry readings and dramatic entertainments. The stage space consisted of about five square feet of slightly raised platform. One night we went to see something there. It was necessary to cross the stage to get to a table. As I crossed the space, a strong urge came to perform *without having anything planned beforehand*, a seemingly ridiculous impulse. I didn't perform that day, but that urge to be spontaneous pointed the way towards the day when I would take the stage and become the conductor in Playback Theatre.

BLOCKS

All too often, spontaneous action remains elusive, supplanted by inappropriate and/or tired responses. An example from the Playback performance text in the appendix is audience-member Gerald's portrayal of the father in Judy's story. Judy had described her father as fiercely angry, but Gerald acted forgiving. Something got in the way for him. He might not have heard Judy's words: “Shocked and outraged... not understanding” (l. 928). Or at the moment of portrayal, even though he had heard the story, he might not have been able to *act* as the story called for. What happens in the scene as enacted is that Gerald as father actually apologizes to his daughter (l. 1015), an interpretation totally out of keeping with the teller's experience.[4]

[4] True, Judy does say in the end “That was just great”(l. 1031) about the rendition, but the fact is, when asked if the audience actors had portrayed her story the way it happened, her reply was unequivocally negative.

At one moment, such a departure from the teller's literal description of what happened might be intuition, while at another, such as this, it was stifled spontaneity.

What frequently blocks the senses from receiving even the most basic input or the brain from giving the body the most simple instructions, are self-conscious thoughts. The internal voice which says, “Don't” may base the argument on life experience--"Remember last time!"--or childhood admoni- tion--“Don't act silly, that's no way to behave!” We may fear to stand out, to make a mistake, to fail, even to succeed. Each of us has our own collection of “should”and “nay" sayers.

In a cultural environment like ours which devalues intuition and physical experience, the voices of admonition can be very strong; spontaneous dramatic action demands we do the very thing that we have been told not to do through most of our lives.

I was once a drama consultant for a nursery school which accepted children classified as developmentally disabled. One young boy, Gary, had a serious behavior problem and often had to be removed from my 30-minute class. However, he became engaged one day as we played “zoo,” choosing to be a bunny curled up in its cage. That Gary, whose short life had been filled with trauma, would opt to be a creature of tenderness and vulnerability did not surprise me, and I suggested “zoo” often after that. One day Gary decided to be a lion. It was a breakthrough for him. For the first time he was able consciously to act not only small and soft, but big, and loud. He was gaining control over his angry feelings, a big step towards more appropriate everyday behavior. I was proud of Gary for being the lion and told him so.

Not long afterwards, I met Gary's adoptive father, a school principal. “I hear you've been playing “zoo" in drama class," he said. When I said I had, he looked at me with a fierce eye: “You've been asking him to be an animal. We spend all our time teaching him how *not* to be an animal!" 

Gary's father, in his fixation on polite comportment, could not see that in playing a lion in drama class Gary was learning control over his outbursts, including how to enter and leave the “angry state.” With such rigidity from parents at home, Gary had little chance, I feared, to maintain the role flexibility he was learning at school. I can only imagine what Gary was told by his parents, but the incessant message, “Don't act like an animal!” is just what many of us have suffered in childhood. These messages come back to haunt us when, later on as adults, we *want* to let go.

Self-consciousness is often masked by conscious thinking. The audience actors in the Playback performance, for example, stopped on the threshold of entering into drama by asking questions: “What's her name, again?” (I. 783)--the hearing sense had closed down-"Can we use sound?”(I. 789)--the model of the previous scenes, all of which used sound, was suddenly forgotten. In fact, the desire to *know* often acts as an impediment to spontaneity. (In our Playback company, the actors never ask questions, but grow accustomed to enacting the story relying on what they *do* know rather than stop them- selves because of what they don't.)

Another common refuge is planning. The unspontaneous actor will often want to decide ahead of time exactly what will happen. A third kind of thinking that can be deadly to spontaneity is analysis. The analytical actor will interpret the scene instead of entering into it, and the portrayal will have a heady, undynamic feel. Don't ask questions, don't plan, don't analyze--honoring these injunctions puts one smack up against the fact of one's resistance to the spontaneous moment.[5] When the actor can't break through, the voice may be stiff and contained; the gestures lifeless and same as last time; the emotional expression either out of reach or out of control (either stuck as the rabbit or a lion that can't stop).

[5] Even scientists, who are ultimately reaching for reason, nevertheless maintain a keen appreciation of the“mindless" aspect of spontaneity. Douglas Hofstadter, writing about how one goes about solving a Rubik's cube, explains: “There is no route to the goal that does not call for a partial but temporary destruction of the visible order up to a given point.” (Hofstadter, 301)

But when the actor is not held back by blocks, then a miracle can take place. It is produced by the whole body. In Playback, it will mean that the actor can intuit untold parts of the teller's story; use the cloth in such a way as to create a deep metaphor for the story's meaning; move the body in a way more expressive than words; or be still. It will enable the actors to move into action with no planning“huddle”common to other forms of improvisation; to cut and expand just the right parts to bring out a story's sense; to make art in a moment of action.

SPONTANEITY'S IMAGE

Grotowski emphasizes that the acting he teaches is not “a collection of skills,” but instead “a *via negativa*, an eradication of blocks." Spolin writes: “It is necessary to become part of the world around us and make it real by touching it, seeing it, feeling it, tasting it, and smelling it." These remarks are compatible with our discussion so far. In fact, emphasis on connecting with primary experience through an open sense apparatus is an important part of most kinds of training for the theatre. The actor “must deal first with relieving whatever difficulties are inherent in himself that negate his freedom of expression and block the capacities he possesses,” writes famous American acting teacher Lee Strasberg.[6]

[6] Grotowski, 8; Spolin, 12; Strasberg in Cole & Chinoy, 624.

Clearly, the literary theatre places strong restrictions on spontaneity: the story is prescribed, the actions planned, the lines of control clear. As one textbook puts it, “theatre games and nontextual scenes should never be used as ends in themselves."

Even the NST, however, though utilizing improvisation more freely, has been wary of fully embracing the idea of spon- taneity. How few have been willing to practice the “simultaneous dramaturgy”described by Boal, or like Dorothy Heathcote, to begin without a fixed plan, “like a gas cooker set at zero?” For example, Roger Bowen, actor for the Compass and Second City Companies, had this to say about their “spot improvs," scenes created from audiencesuggestions:“In content it is the most superficial of the things we do. Certainly a whole evening of complete improvisation wouldn't add up to anything." And Schechner, writing in 1969, asserts: “Improv is a tool in finding and making a mise-en-scene and a score [text]. It can be deadly when you are trying to maintain a score during performances. For a performer to be free to let his feelings flow during a performance, he must know that for himself, and for his partners, each detail of the score must be strictly adhered to... That is the only way.”[7]

[7] See the chapter on improvisation in Albright & Albright, 60-76; Dorothy Heathcote, quoted in *Three Looms Waiting*, a film about her work; Boal, 132; Sweet, 38; Schechner 1969, 29.

Improvisational performance is a litmus test of spontaneity. Such views as those cited above indicate a deep-seated opposition to unplanned action. In my experience, spontaneity demands a full commitment, no matter what the test. It quickly vanishes in the face of dogmatic ideas, fixed roles, rigid discipline, or a structure of authority that discourages the unexpected.

In the search for spontaneity, regressive behavior is a necessary and healthy phenomenon, a process of transition from the everyday to the actor's world, a loosening up of the improvisational muscles. It is not congruent with a view towards theatre work, such as Grotowski's, that requires constant seriousness (“Private ideas of fun," he writes, “have no place in the actor's calling.”[8]). It would appear that there is a strong connection betweenspontaneity, playfulness, and even eroticism. Spontaneity is juicy. Spontaneity is hot, as Artaud knew. In fact, when I work with a new company, I look to taste, smell and feel a certain degree of earthiness. This tells me an atmosphere of spontaneity is present.

[8] Grotowski, 258.

Keith Johnstone writes that there are three sources of blocked spontaneity: fear of being unoriginal, fear of being crazy, and fear of being obscene.[9] Conversely, when a group of people really do let their spontaneous responses flow, I have found they often turn quite childish, scatological-and often creative. There is tremendous energy in releasing the taboos. Many illustrative memories come back to me. At the end of our first decade we returned to the church where we first practiced and gave in to an irresistible urge to clown in the church cemetery. On my birthday, the actors presented me with a human cake, out of which came Michael, in sequined nipples and g-string. Once on Carolyn and Vince's birthday, we spontaneously abandoned rehearsal and took them off, blind- folded, to a hot tub. More often than not, our rehearsals began with rude banter and a school-kid disregard of the director.

[9] See Johnstone, chapter 3, “Spontaneity."

In talking about subconscious impulses, we are dealing with a subject which has been hotly debated throughout this century: followers of Freud have argued for the pervasiveness of repression and sublimation; the behaviorists, for the dominance of learned behaviors. In opposition, there has been a vocal group of writers who see the unconscious in a positive light, whether it be called collective unconscious (Jung), fantasy (Moreno), or inspiration(Stanislavski). The theory of spontaneity draws on this latter position, seeing creative possibility in going beyond the boundaries of past behavior and rationality.

The call of spontaneity helps explain why actors do act wild-especially in comparison with restrained, more “adult” members of society, who are conditioned to lives of habit and obedience. The actor, of course, is entering into a “nonadult” state for a totally serious purpose, and the procedure is not without risk. There is always the prospect that in flirting with taboo, we will get caught, lose our grounding, and in some way never be able to “return."

In NST we wish not to banish these low elements, but to harness them. For in an instant, low can become high, silly can turn sublime; Peter Brook, to his credit, has always been attracted to the “rough” theatre precisely because it can become “holy."[10]

[10] Heilpern comments on his experience with Peter Brook in Africa: “The actor who fails to link the high with the low will not gain access into the invisible world, nor will his audience." Brook, of course, has throughout his career championed the mixture of formal and informal elements that characterized Elizabethan theatre. See Heilper, 292, and Brook 1968.

How do we train to become more spontaneous? We loosen the grip of rationality.We do a great deal of physical, nonverbal work to take us out of the thinking mode and expand our capacity to move, sound, and in general respond as young Gary's father deplored-as *animals*. We practice acting in a wide variety of roles, particularly in those where we block. We also do not hesitate to share about and even deal therapeutically at times with the background of personal experience which prevents us from acting with spontaneity. And all the while, we follow a spontaneous approach to our work process rather than a rigidly prescribed one.

The spontaneity approach does not deny the value of sophistication. However, it recognizes that creativity does not have to do so much with knowledge as with meeting the challenge of the moment with our full selves, including the animal part of us. With his motley dress, juggling balls, and glossolalia, the fool brought a vital element to oral society, joining the artisan and the priest, as Ken Feit puts it, in a 'conspiracy of meaning."[11] One can stop and wonder what exactly that meaning is...or one can not ask, and instead be like the fool, the child, the rabbit, and the lion.

[11] Feit, 97-108. Feit, a modern fool who touched many people, disappeared driving his car one day out in the sky-filled Midwest.
