# AFTERWORD A CHALLENGE FOR THE CITIZEN ACTOR

When I had the vision of people seeing their real stories dramatized by actors from their own communities, it was a fateful moment. But I never imagined the little group we started in 1975 in rural New York State would engender a movement with followers on every continent, Nor did I imagine that *Acts of Service* would still be in demand almost a decade after its publication. Although it did not make sense to revise the entire text--fifteen years have gone by since the first draft was typed--I did want to comment on the book from today's vantage point.

How has *Acts of Service* stood up to time?

Qur experience over the last decade has only emphasized the importance of the initial section on orality, in particular the preliterary drama paradigm as described in chapter 3. In time, of course, we have become much more sophisticated in our use of it. For example,we continue to adapt to circumstances as *environmental attunement* suggests. However, we have learned--sometimes the hard way--the minimum environmental conditions for maintaining the playback theatre ritual.[1] We are flexible, but not infinitely flexible. We will perform for any group of people who sincerely want to listen to each other's stories. But we will usually refuse to do so for groups that are too large, to take one example, because we know by now that the integrity of the work depends on aquality of intimacy that is simply not possible with several hundred people in the room, no matter how sophisticated a sound system we may be offered.

[1] I am adopting a change in the common usage of the termplayback theatre: it is now generally written in lower case letterswhen referring to the form itself, and capitalized when referring to acompany, e.g., Asheville Playback Theatre.

Ritual, hardly touched on in *Acts of Service*, is core to playback theatre. It is the playback theatre ritual--the teller comes to the chair (and the teller stays in the chair); the actor sstand when chosen; the conductor says, “Let's watch” when the interview is over, and so forth--that provides a sense of safety for the audience. A potential teller knows that no matter what her or his story, it will be treated in this measured, consistent way. The actors will contain it in their ritual process. They will start the enactment cleanly. They will conclude it cleanly. The sequence will end with the focus back on the teller, as the conductor seeks a final acknowledgment or comment. It will never be taken away from the teller and made merely a provocation for clever actors.

The sameness of the ritual allows, paradoxically, for tellers to take risks, often surprising themselves by the story that emerges. A truly deep story becomes a risk for everyone. The emotions become intense. Playback actors spend many years training to enact such stories without losing the ritual. Such mastery permits playback theatre to be at its strongest and most socially useful. Witnessing in a dramatic form our neighbor's experience can build communities that have been torn asunder. It can even take a step towards redeeming history.[2]

[2] For more on deep stories and the playback theatre ritual, see *Gathering Voices: Essays on Playback Theatre*, Heinrich Dauber & Jonathan Fox, Eds. (New York: Tusitala, 1997).

Insofar as playback's purpose is to translate individual experience into a form understandable by the collective, it is always concerned with “interculcural” experience. But the growth of playback has also tested the viability of the idea in settings far afield from playback's origins in upstate New York. It turns out that playback theatre appeals to groups with a very different stance towards personal revelation than in the United States, such as in Japan, or India, or Hungary, or Burundi, or Fiji--all countries where playback theatre is now practiced. At the same time, each culture adapts the forms. Which is as it should be. But such adaptations will always raise a further question: what of the playback ritual is core to the basic idea and must be retained? From the start, we have never insisted on controlling how playback theatre is used. We have come to appreciate, however, how important a good training is (since it is people's personal stories we are dealing with). It is remarkable the extent to which, to date, new practitioners have accepted this need for a period of apprenticeship and sought tutelage before launching their own playback endeavors. Without it, they risk failure, or worse, harm to their tellers andaudiences.[3]

[3] For specific information on playback experience in different settings throughout the world, see INTERPLAY, the newsletter ofthe International Playback Theatre Network [IPTN] at www.playbacknet.org.

The *redressive* nature of playback theatre (returning to the preliterary drama paradigm) has always been a challenging negotiation in dealing with today's social organizations. Mental health professionals easily fall into a trap of thinking of playback theatre as only therapy, while those outside the therapeutic domain, out of fearfuness, may mistakenly think the same. “Playback is therapeutic, but not therapy," we say, “although it is used at times as an adjunctive therapeutic technique in mental health settings." How complex! Sometimes I have said, “We are trying to create a context similar to that of a traditional society, where there is no clear separation between art and healing." How uncomfortably New Age! It is not simply a matter of language use. In fact we are constantly educating organizations to appreciate the value of a communal healing ceremony.

One of my original intentions in writing Acts ofServicebegun in the mid-eighties-was to make the general argumentfor nonscripted theatre, and this is why playback theatre is cited as merely the principal example throughout the book rather than the sole focus. Reviewing the section on different types of nonscripted theatre, the scene has shifted. Today the most widespread approach is Augusto Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed (TO). Boal, of course, is mentioned many times in this book, but mostly for his ideas rather than his practice. Since *Acts of Service* was first published, TO *animateurs* have brought their highly participatory approach to many mainstream segments of society throughout the world. TO's emphasis on the citizen actor is similar to playback's. But there are differences: where playback invites catharsis, TO rejects it; where playback allows an often unconscious dialogue through narrative icons, TO seeks conscious dialogue and concrete solutions.

Because of the compatibility of purpose, some groups are successfully performing with a format that is part TO and part playback theatre. In this and other ways, as communities learn to value audience participation, shared creativity, and social awareness, the widespread success of TO is helping to pave the way for playback theatre. Other developments have also contributed to a more benign reception, notably the popularity of autobiographical performance that emerged beginning in the 1980s.

Playback theatre is also allied with the theatre for development movement (TFD), a grass-roots approach practiced principally in Africa. TFD seeks, through theatre, to engage ordinary people in formulating their own answers to problems of social policy, and channeling these outcomes to decision makers. Originating with British academics, and seeded by western funds,TFD seeks to integrate oral traditional culture and modern approaches to problem solving and policy formulation.

Another originator of a form of NST that has gained widespread currency is Keith Johnstone. Johnstone has sought to give theatre the appeal of sport. His Theatresports, in which teams of improvisers compete for audience approval, is now practiced in many cities. He has even conceived of a format very close to playback theatre called Lifegame, in which a "guest's" life story is elicited by an onstage “interviewer,” then portrayed in segments by improvising actors. This approach, as developed by a British team, has achieved some commercial theatre success. Lifegame and playback theatre, while look-alikes, are hardly similar under the skin: Lifegame is strictly entertainment, whereas playback has always been more than entertainment. For example, the evening we saw Lifegame in New York City a major scene was built from the guest's account of his first kiss. A first kiss is always food for anamusing enactment, but there was no evident pursuit on the part of the performers of the more elusive but profound meaning of a life, and what its connections might be to the members of the audience and their own stories.

The chapters on NST production, with their emphasis on spontaneity, the citizen actor, the multimodal director, and company life hold up today. What is missing here is the social development part of the playback enterprise. By this I mean not the actual performance itself, but what goes into arranging it beforehand--the extensive networking and negotiation required to bring playback where it is most needed and will be most effective.

There continues to be a tradition of public performing among many playback theatre companies, especially in the western world. But performances that take place in the context of a specific organization or community group have, if anything, taken more focus than in the early years. For example, there has been an interest in the playback community in creating teams to perform "emergency playback theatre" following natural disasters or civic crises. We know how important it is, at the right moment during a crisis, to tell the story, and we know what a good medium playback is for that. But to achieve a success with emergency playback, we need special training in trauma work. We also need to achieve acceptance by the agencies responsible for dealing with these situations, such as the Red Cross, or in the USA, the Federal Emergency Management Association. Playback performers are edging towards such bold objectives and developing the nontheatrical skills to do so.

In the section on playback theatre and education in *Acts of Service*, I focused mainly on the playback actor. My interest has for some years now expanded to embrace the subject of playback theatre's usefulness to academics who may not themselves be actors. Playback performers need trained minds to help them understand what makes playback theatre effective and what are its uses in the broadest sense. With playback's steady growth, it has emerged as a topic of study and practice in the university, an important development, Playback theatre is now taught in a variety of university departments, including education, arts in education, communications, performance studies, theatre, and drama therapy.

J.L. Moreno wrote: “The first step to be taken must be with the consent and the cooperation of the individuals concerned. It must be made by them as if it were their own project--their own design for living. There is no other way imaginable which can enlist the spontaneity, the critical intelligence and the enthusiasm of grown up, thinking people."[4] Moreno was writing about group process and social research, but this statement also applies to playback theatre. In our early years we pursued a goal of creating a mood that invited maximum participation, believing that out of such enthusiasm would ensue a will for deep dialogue. Like TO, TFD, and Theatresports we sought in our own way to democratize the theatre process.

[4] *Sociometry, Experimental Method, and the Science of Society*, 1951, p. 187.

Democracy, however, is no easy process, especially when wedded to a concern for social justice. As playback theatre practice has matured, there has been a shift in focus from the individual teller to the group-as-teller. Indicative of this trend is our frequent reference these days to the “red thread," those links between stories, and the idea of playback as community dialogue.[5]

[5] See Folma Hoesch, “The Red Thread," and my “A Ritualfor our Time" in Gathering Voices.

And even beyond, for many of us are now equally concerned with what might be called the invisible, or buried, thread, For example, who does not get a chance to tell their story, and why? Who holds back, and why? Who would never come to a playback show, and why? And who would not audition to be a playback actor, and why? The patterns of participation relate directly to the society-at-large, and in a country like the US, it must be said, patterns of participation reflect its endemic racism. Thus the playback team, just to perform their art as they wish, must be agents for social change, working to reverse the trend of history. This is a big and daunting task. It requires special acting and conducting skills, and it tends to spill over to other parts of one's life. It has taken us out of the rehearsal space, where by now we feel we have learned our method, and into the community, where we have work to do.

Jonathan Fox,
New Paltz, January, 2003
