# CHAPTER FOURTEEN COMPANY LIFE

So far I have made references to my own and other Playback Theatre "companies." These are groups that meet regularly on an ongoing basis to practice and perform. They come in many different shapes and sizes, however, and for the most part are organized quite differently from a typical company in the literary theatre, which organizes around a single play and then disbands. Some are incorporated legally; many are not (in the case of Playback, even though all practice an approach developed by myself and the original company, there is no controlling financial interest--no central company; we do have a support organization with membership and elected officers).

In fact, it is not necessary to be part of a company to practice the Playback Theatre idea; there are many individual practitioners, for example, who facilitate their client groups, whether school students, company personnel, or psychiatric patients, to act out stories for each other. Nevertheless, there is something special about a *company*. It is something I can feel in the air the minute I walk into the room. There's an energy, an urgency to make the most out of the moment. I look around at the people gathered there. They may not even be in action yet. I may never have met them before. But I smile inwardly, saying to myself, “This is a theatre company!”

In contrast, I have visited groups which, though they may lay claim to it, are not a theatre company. There is a  distance between individuals and a feeling of negative intentionality一refusal to engage in this or that activity, including performing; a desire not to spend too much time at it; of not wanting to get too personally intense.

The word “company,” of course, describes any band of performers, with an emphasis on “an association of persons for a joint purpose." Nevertheless, theatre is also likely to be labor- intensive, interactive, and emotional-aspects which highlight the “fellowship” connotation of the word. What is distinctive about literary theatre companies is that despite the energetic nature of the product development stage, the period of delivery is liable to be very short. The tradition in literary theatre is to collect a group of people for the production of a particular play and dissolve it at the end of the run. A theatre*company which stays intact for more than one play (the repertory company) is exceptional, at least in the United States. Even though its foothold has been precarious, however, the idea of the repertory company has exerted an appeal among theatre professionals. And there have been notable achievements. The Group Theatre and the Civic Repertory Theatre in the 1930s proved that repertory was a viable means of mounting serious plays at a high artistic level. Today, this function has been taken over by the repertory theatres of certain universities, especially Harvard and Yale. The repertory idea's failure to gain energy during the I940s and 1950s fueled the ex- perimental movement, whose ardent emphasis on ensemble was as much an answer to literary theatre's failure as it was an acceptance of the communal nature of oral culture.[1]

[1] See Sainer, 13. The American system of providing limited official subsidy for arts cannot be the whole explanation for repertory's hard battle. Could the American individualistic credo also be significant in discouraging a form of organization that requires group values and the skills to foster them? The emergence of the regional theatre movement since World War II perhaps reflects an interest in the community-based theatrical organization, in which there is an overall continuity of personnel, but it is not clear to what extent regional theatre productions honor the idea of “permanent company." See Baumol & Bowen, 27-8.

In the NST, the norm is for theatre groups to be assembled on more than a one-production basis. Nevertheless,NST groups do not for the most part fit under the rubric of "repertory company" because their productions are characterized by a repetitive allegiance to form rather than a diverse series of plays. Thus “company” in the NST context takes on a different meaning.

First, it implies *continuity*. It often takes explaining for audience members to understand that a Playback Theatre company is not together just for the show, or even for the season, but on a “permanent" basis, in which the average tenure of a performer will last for years.

The second vital connotation for “company” in the NST is a strong sense of *collaboration*. In his book on group psychotherapy, Irvin Yalom suggests that the defining charac- teristic of groups is cohesion, “a sense of belonging or 'we-ness' that groups of individuals feel under certain circumstances." Thus we can contrast a group from an aggregate, a collection of persons who maintain interpersonal distance, and say that in the NST, especially, a theatre company is a group.[2]

[2] Yalom, 46.

Is performing a defining characteristic for a theatre company? I have suggested already that self-reflexive action is inherent in a full concept of spontaneity, and that the pressure of performing forges cohesiveness, perhaps for the very reason that it is taking the theatrical idea to its limit. I can imagine a company which does not perform, but I have never seen one. Thus for practical purposes, we can define a theatre company as an ongoing performing group.

A group of individuals in open-ended, intimate relationship sounds very much like a *family*, and in many ways, the analogy is apt. A theatre company, like a family, will share fun, food, and common tasks. It will provide support, acceptance, love. It will give permission to be apart. And it will erupt in conflict. A good theatre company, like a good family, will have a mechanism for airing and resolving differences.

With its clear separation between professional and personal, the kind of cohesion we are talking about is a mere by-product in the literary theatre. In fact, there are those teachers and directors who would find too much personal interaction threatening to artistic purpose. Lee Strasberg, as we observed earlier, gave positive emphasis to a discrepancy between Program and Mood and asserted that he “didn't care” about conflict in the group.[3]

[3] Quoted in Gassner, 327.

Let me offer an example of a different attitude to Mood-
Program incongruence:

We are three-quarters of the way through a week-long
Playback Theatre workshop. The participants are beginning a day of preparations for an end-of-week performance. This performance exerts Program pressure on the participants. There is a lot to be done in a short time. I am the teacher. I also notice that one of the men, Joe, seems angry. I decide to begin the day's work by asking each person to say how they are--an innocent question, perhaps, but one which I hope will give Joe a chance to relax and relate. When it is his turn he declares: “I don't want to say how I am. I don't want to get into it. I think it's better if I just leave now."

How should I proceed? He is not an easy fellow, this Joe.
He talks more than he listens. But I invite him to say more. It seems the evening before, after the day's work had ended, many group members rejected him. I invite him to unburden. He does. He yells, he screams, he cries. He says he is walking out. Frank, one of the individuals who had spurned him, starts to cry. "If you only know how much I want love,” he says. Now others cry, moved by the sight of two men holding each other. By the end, we are in touch with Joe's deep and admirable reason for being among us, and so is he. Gone is his talk of leaving.

This whole procedure has taken over an hour. We even need a break afterwards because the emotion has been so strong. But now there is an elation among us, a sense that nothing matters because we have each other, a tremendous feeling of power and confidence. Like Black Elk, we can savor the greenness of the world and the wideness of the sacred day. And when the work starts, it is wonderfully energetic and spontaneous--as are the performances on the following day.

Like families, the life of theatre companies also goes through stages, and it is a particular challenge both to be aware of the passages and to accept them. One difficulty is that the life stages of a theatre company are not always clearly marked, nor do we have ancient rituals for marking them, such as baptism, confirmation, and marriage. The absence of such rituals does not diminish their necessity.

Of course, even NST theatre companies are not families. Particularly if their Program is a strong one, they are instrumental associations, with fluctuating memberships and an intentional existence. They can always disband permanently. New members will join at a faster rate than in families. The size usually is fixed at a relatively constant figure related to Program needs.

*Auditions*. The audition procedure customary for plays is not a suitable mechanism for admitting new actors into NST companies. It is essential to see more than a face and hear more than a monologue for a position which will last indefinitely and in which the actor's personhood is as important as his or her persona.

In keeping with NST's preliterary roots, more appropriate, perhaps, is the idea of apprenticeship. In this approach an apprentice actor studies the form over a long period, until he or she is deemed worthy to be admitted into the artisans'circle. My sense is that the apprentice approach can be developed and adapted to contemporary needs more than it has been. Nonscripted theatre groups have been held back, on the one hand, by a failure of conception, in which they accept inappropriate audition practices of literary theatre, and on the other, by a failure of program priority, in which they slight the complex organizational demands of a long-term, well-integrated apprentice program.[4]

[4] “Apprentice” is a not unfamiliar word in the literary theatre. In the USA, it most often refers to a month or two's work in summer stock, or a season's service in a repertory company (of which, as we have pointed out, there are very few). Thus as a concept it rather pays lip service to the meaning of the term than fulflls it.

In practice, individual companies often find their own amalgamation of the audition and apprenticeapproaches. In different Playback Theatre groups for example, practices vary considerably. They include invitations to selected individuals to join practice sessions for a certain period, after which a decision is made; weekend workshop-auditions which par- ticipants pay for, after which some are selected for the company; a workshop-audition which both familiar students and total newcomers are encouraged to attend, followed by call-back and final selection; and open admission, upon acceptance of certain rules of participation.

*Orientation*. Adjustment to membership in an NST company is difficult. The degree of interpersonal closeness shared by the members cannot help but be intimidating to a newcomer. To learn the form well takes time; serious feelings of inadequacy often follow the triumph of admission to the group. In a culture which fails to teach the skills for dealing with Mood considerations, we are liable to ignore the needs of newcomers as we blithely progress with the Program. Nevertheless, it is important to deal well with the question of how to integrate new members.

*Termination*. Members inevitably will leave the group, and when they do, it is always highly significant for everyone. After two years of stellar work in our company, Scott, the youngest actor, decided to leave. Scott is a talented performer. I also felt like a big brother to him. So his departure was a great loss. Naturally, we had a good-bye party for him, but in addition some of us took part in a special session, facilitated by a knowing friend, in which we shared what we had learned from each other. This amount of time spent in farewell, and the quality of it, enabled us as a group to accept Scott's departure, and I was able to let him go to make his own way in the world.

Our human leaning toward stability, and subsequent resentment of change, can lead us to ignore the imperatives of membership changes as well as to slight the hellos and good- byes. This question comes up most urgently when faced with the problem of replacing personnel. Actors will need to move on; actors will become tired. It is important to have others, already familiar with the form, already qualified, ready to step in. Such preparatory work must be accomplished at the height of a stable period in the knowledge that change must come. Thus a flux of personnel must be expected and prepared for, including appropriate procedures of welcome and farewell.

## THE POWER OF THE NST LEADER

As we have emphasized in earlier chapters, the leader of an
NST group is liable to be multifunctional. The director, the conductor, even the founder may be embodied in one in- dividual. Certain characteristics of NST company life stem from this fact. There frequently comes a time in the maturation of a company when the charismatic energy of a founding director is no longer as needed as a more rational, delegated management approach. Often the leader cannot adjust to this transition. Because of the leader's central importance, it also follows that his or her leaving is a much harder phenomenon to deal with than an actor's. The leader may be unique; often an entire company falls when the leader needs a change.

The authority, power, prestige, and creative opportunity inherent in NST leadership can also create power struggles.
