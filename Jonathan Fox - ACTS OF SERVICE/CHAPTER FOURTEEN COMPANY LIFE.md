# CHAPTER FOURTEEN COMPANY LIFE

So far I have made references to my own and other Playback Theatre "companies." These are groups that meet regularly on an ongoing basis to practice and perform. They come in many different shapes and sizes, however, and for the most part are organized quite differently from a typical company in the literary theatre, which organizes around a single play and then disbands. Some are incorporated legally; many are not (in the case of Playback, even though all practice an approach developed by myself and the original company, there is no controlling financial interest--no central company; we do have a support organization with membership and elected officers).

In fact, it is not necessary to be part of a company to practice the Playback Theatre idea; there are many individual practitioners, for example, who facilitate their client groups, whether school students, company personnel, or psychiatric patients, to act out stories for each other. Nevertheless, there is something special about a *company*. It is something I can feel in the air the minute I walk into the room. There's an energy, an urgency to make the most out of the moment. I look around at the people gathered there. They may not even be in action yet. I may never have met them before. But I smile inwardly, saying to myself, “This is a theatre company!”

In contrast, I have visited groups which, though they may lay claim to it, are not a theatre company. There is a  distance between individuals and a feeling of negative intentionality一refusal to engage in this or that activity, including performing; a desire not to spend too much time at it; of not wanting to get too personally intense.

The word “company,” of course, describes any band of performers, with an emphasis on “an association of persons for a joint purpose." Nevertheless, theatre is also likely to be labor- intensive, interactive, and emotional-aspects which highlight the “fellowship” connotation of the word. What is distinctive about literary theatre companies is that despite the energetic nature of the product development stage, the period of delivery is liable to be very short. The tradition in literary theatre is to collect a group of people for the production of a particular play and dissolve it at the end of the run. A theatre*company which stays intact for more than one play (the repertory company) is exceptional, at least in the United States. Even though its foothold has been precarious, however, the idea of the repertory company has exerted an appeal among theatre professionals. And there have been notable achievements. The Group Theatre and the Civic Repertory Theatre in the 1930s proved that repertory was a viable means of mounting serious plays at a high artistic level. Today, this function has been taken over by the repertory theatres of certain universities, especially Harvard and Yale. The repertory idea's failure to gain energy during the I940s and 1950s fueled the ex- perimental movement, whose ardent emphasis on ensemble was as much an answer to literary theatre's failure as it was an acceptance of the communal nature of oral culture.[1]

[1] See Sainer, 13. The American system of providing limited official subsidy for arts cannot be the whole explanation for repertory's hard battle. Could the American individualistic credo also be significant in discouraging a form of organization that requires group values and the skills to foster them? The emergence of the regional theatre movement since World War II perhaps reflects an interest in the community-based theatrical organization, in which there is an overall continuity of personnel, but it is not clear to what extent regional theatre productions honor the idea of “permanent company." See Baumol & Bowen, 27-8.

In the NST, the norm is for theatre groups to be assembled on more than a one-production basis. Nevertheless,NST groups do not for the most part fit under the rubric of "repertory company" because their productions are characterized by a repetitive allegiance to form rather than a diverse series of plays. Thus “company” in the NST context takes on a different meaning.

First, it implies *continuity*. It often takes explaining for audience members to understand that a Playback Theatre company is not together just for the show, or even for the season, but on a “permanent" basis, in which the average tenure of a performer will last for years.

The second vital connotation for “company” in the NST is a strong sense of *collaboration*. In his book on group psychotherapy, Irvin Yalom suggests that the defining charac- teristic of groups is cohesion, “a sense of belonging or 'we-ness' that groups of individuals feel under certain circumstances." Thus we can contrast a group from an aggregate, a collection of persons who maintain interpersonal distance, and say that in the NST, especially, a theatre company is a group.[2]

[2] Yalom, 46.

Is performing a defining characteristic for a theatre company? I have suggested already that self-reflexive action is inherent in a full concept of spontaneity, and that the pressure of performing forges cohesiveness, perhaps for the very reason that it is taking the theatrical idea to its limit. I can imagine a company which does not perform, but I have never seen one. Thus for practical purposes, we can define a theatre company as an ongoing performing group.

A group of individuals in open-ended, intimate relationship sounds very much like a *family*, and in many ways, the analogy is apt. A theatre company, like a family, will share fun, food, and common tasks. It will provide support, acceptance, love. It will give permission to be apart. And it will erupt in conflict. A good theatre company, like a good family, will have a mechanism for airing and resolving differences.

With its clear separation between professional and personal, the kind of cohesion we are talking about is a mere by-product in the literary theatre. In fact, there are those teachers and directors who would find too much personal interaction threatening to artistic purpose. Lee Strasberg, as we observed earlier, gave positive emphasis to a discrepancy between Program and Mood and asserted that he “didn't care” about conflict in the group.[3]

[3] Quoted in Gassner, 327.

Let me offer an example of a different attitude to Mood-
Program incongruence:

We are three-quarters of the way through a week-long
Playback Theatre workshop. The participants are beginning a day of preparations for an end-of-week performance. This performance exerts Program pressure on the participants. There is a lot to be done in a short time. I am the teacher. I also notice that one of the men, Joe, seems angry. I decide to begin the day's work by asking each person to say how they are--an innocent question, perhaps, but one which I hope will give Joe a chance to relax and relate. When it is his turn he declares: “I don't want to say how I am. I don't want to get into it. I think it's better if I just leave now."

How should I proceed? He is not an easy fellow, this Joe.
He talks more than he listens. But I invite him to say more. It seems the evening before, after the day's work had ended, many group members rejected him. I invite him to unburden. He does. He yells, he screams, he cries. He says he is walking out. Frank, one of the individuals who had spurned him, starts to cry. "If you only know how much I want love,” he says. Now others cry, moved by the sight of two men holding each other. By the end, we are in touch with Joe's deep and admirable reason for being among us, and so is he. Gone is his talk of leaving.

This whole procedure has taken over an hour. We even need a break afterwards because the emotion has been so strong. But now there is an elation among us, a sense that nothing matters because we have each other, a tremendous feeling of power and confidence. Like Black Elk, we can savor the greenness of the world and the wideness of the sacred day. And when the work starts, it is wonderfully energetic and spontaneous--as are the performances on the following day.

Like families, the life of theatre companies also goes through stages, and it is a particular challenge both to be aware of the passages and to accept them. One difficulty is that the life stages of a theatre company are not always clearly marked, nor do we have ancient rituals for marking them, such as baptism, confirmation, and marriage. The absence of such rituals does not diminish their necessity.

Of course, even NST theatre companies are not families. Particularly if their Program is a strong one, they are instrumental associations, with fluctuating memberships and an intentional existence. They can always disband permanently. New members will join at a faster rate than in families. The size usually is fixed at a relatively constant figure related to Program needs.

*Auditions*. The audition procedure customary for plays is not a suitable mechanism for admitting new actors into NST companies. It is essential to see more than a face and hear more than a monologue for a position which will last indefinitely and in which the actor's personhood is as important as his or her persona.

In keeping with NST's preliterary roots, more appropriate, perhaps, is the idea of apprenticeship. In this approach an apprentice actor studies the form over a long period, until he or she is deemed worthy to be admitted into the artisans'circle. My sense is that the apprentice approach can be developed and adapted to contemporary needs more than it has been. Nonscripted theatre groups have been held back, on the one hand, by a failure of conception, in which they accept inappropriate audition practices of literary theatre, and on the other, by a failure of program priority, in which they slight the complex organizational demands of a long-term, well-integrated apprentice program.[4]

[4] “Apprentice” is a not unfamiliar word in the literary theatre. In the USA, it most often refers to a month or two's work in summer stock, or a season's service in a repertory company (of which, as we have pointed out, there are very few). Thus as a concept it rather pays lip service to the meaning of the term than fulflls it.

In practice, individual companies often find their own amalgamation of the audition and apprenticeapproaches. In different Playback Theatre groups for example, practices vary considerably. They include invitations to selected individuals to join practice sessions for a certain period, after which a decision is made; weekend workshop-auditions which par- ticipants pay for, after which some are selected for the company; a workshop-audition which both familiar students and total newcomers are encouraged to attend, followed by call-back and final selection; and open admission, upon acceptance of certain rules of participation.

*Orientation*. Adjustment to membership in an NST company is difficult. The degree of interpersonal closeness shared by the members cannot help but be intimidating to a newcomer. To learn the form well takes time; serious feelings of inadequacy often follow the triumph of admission to the group. In a culture which fails to teach the skills for dealing with Mood considerations, we are liable to ignore the needs of newcomers as we blithely progress with the Program. Nevertheless, it is important to deal well with the question of how to integrate new members.

*Termination*. Members inevitably will leave the group, and when they do, it is always highly significant for everyone. After two years of stellar work in our company, Scott, the youngest actor, decided to leave. Scott is a talented performer. I also felt like a big brother to him. So his departure was a great loss. Naturally, we had a good-bye party for him, but in addition some of us took part in a special session, facilitated by a knowing friend, in which we shared what we had learned from each other. This amount of time spent in farewell, and the quality of it, enabled us as a group to accept Scott's departure, and I was able to let him go to make his own way in the world.

Our human leaning toward stability, and subsequent resentment of change, can lead us to ignore the imperatives of membership changes as well as to slight the hellos and good- byes. This question comes up most urgently when faced with the problem of replacing personnel. Actors will need to move on; actors will become tired. It is important to have others, already familiar with the form, already qualified, ready to step in. Such preparatory work must be accomplished at the height of a stable period in the knowledge that change must come. Thus a flux of personnel must be expected and prepared for, including appropriate procedures of welcome and farewell.

## THE POWER OF THE NST LEADER

As we have emphasized in earlier chapters, the leader of an
NST group is liable to be multifunctional. The director, the conductor, even the founder may be embodied in one in- dividual. Certain characteristics of NST company life stem from this fact. There frequently comes a time in the maturation of a company when the charismatic energy of a founding director is no longer as needed as a more rational, delegated management approach. Often the leader cannot adjust to this transition. Because of the leader's central importance, it also follows that his or her leaving is a much harder phenomenon to deal with than an actor's. The leader may be unique; often an entire company falls when the leader needs a change.

The authority, power, prestige, and creative opportunity inherent in NST leadership can also create power struggles. The ongoing nature of NST companies argues that there must be a mechanism for change of leaders just as for performers[5]

[5] It would be of interest to examine the life and politics of nonscripted theatre companies in terms of Turner's social drama paradigm, originally formulated from study of nonliterary communities in Africa. Turner claims validity for the paradigm in modern as well as preliterary contexts, but the examination of a nonliterary modern institution in light of Turner's model might provide some answers for how NST institutions might best deal with conflict and change.

The role of leader of an NST company is a difficult role to fill. The holistic perspective of NST demands that the decisions which face a company will have a personal as well as artistic focus. And insofar as the purpose is redressive, a group will attempt to move towards realization of transformative experience both in its productions and its process.

Clearly the leader can either foster or block progress towards this ambitious goal. Preliterarysociety often con- sidered such figures intermediaries between the human and “more than human," or divine, realms. They paid special respect to those with the wisdom, skill, and courage to regularly lead their people through liminal ordeals towards understanding and health.

Now actors fulfill this role, too, as Cole has emphasized when he writes about actors'impact on audiences. But the director-conductor, by virtue of the special focus and responsibility both in practice and performance, is in a particularly influential position.[6]

[6] Cole, 70-73.

Furthermore, the NSTtendency towards a blending of distinctions between company and community suggests that the leader will need to be held in high esteem not only by his most intimate circle, but also in the larger circles of audience, students, and general public.

It is important that the leader be able to slough off the mantle of authority outside the framework of theatre work and be an ordinary person. Nevertheless, the down-home nature of NST work pushes out the boundaries of the leader's duties. An actor may be in a crisis. A spectator-neighbor may phone with a comment. A detail will demand attention. From such service comes a special fatigue, and a special loneliness. It is one thing to decry authoritarianism in NST leadership, but to suggest, as does Viola Spolin, that “true personal freedom and self- expression can flower only in an atmosphere where attitudes permit equality between students and teacher” naively ignores the “other-ness" of the leader. Finding the balance between true authority and democracy is hard enough when exposed to a group only briefly, but when the exposure is intense and longstanding, the leader must be able to marshall considerable resources indeed. A bald way to make my point draws on Moreno's terminology: to be a leader in the NST, you cannot be an isolate, you have to be a chosen leader.

It is time for some examples:

Judy, a member of the original companyin New York, played a special role in the social sphere. Actors often passed through her home before or after practice. Judy had something special that others responded to--an amazing willingness to be accepting, an ability to be truly in the moment, and a deep commitment to human growth. When the original company slowed down, a new Playback group coalesced around her because she was the center of a large network of people. Her house, and the workshop space she had fixed up in its large garage, was already a living symbol of Playback interactivity; it became the home of the new group.

Martin and Marilyn are a New Zealand husband-and-wife team who possess both an undying involvement in theatre and a heightened sense of service that includes personal hospitality. They extend their parent-like caring and encouragement outward through many circles of people. At time of writing, they have now been the leaders of a Playback company for seven years.

Mary, Australian founder of the Playback company in her city, is a person with wide-ranging interests and thus a magnet for diverse collectives. Mary is both a healer and an artist, profoundly wholesome but unafraid to be irreverent. She reaches for what she wants, accepts change, yet has a high sense of responsibility for others. Her particular combination of personal gifts give her a wisdom which others seek, and she is an inspiring role model.

The kind of leader I am talking about is not the professional guru who in fact may be concealing deep personal neediness by specializing in the neediness of dependent others. Rather he or she is the truly balanced individual who accepts this position of special responsibility and encourages both artistic and personal autonomy in others. In sum, it will not work to have charisma without substance, or substance without charisma. The ideal NST leader possesses a high measure of both.

Now, what about an individual who might aspire to this kind of leadership, but who lacks the stature? This is a difficult issue for a person to face. One's training for leadership might be tailored to enrich weak areas, but there are limitations to this developmental approach. Not everybody, no matter how earnest their motivation, can marshall the qualities for NST leadership. The irony is that while actors are usually selected by a group process (rich in possibility for measuring self-perception against the assessment of others), NST leaders, at least at the company start-up level, are usually self-selected. Accurate self-knowledge is essential so that an aspiring NST leader can pick a context suitable to his or her particular degree of shamanism. The most ambitious form of NST leadership is probably founding a company in a new community. Short of that, there are many other opportunities. To take the Playback Theatre world as an example, people lead Playback groups which do not perform for the public. There are Playback groups in which membership is ongoing and by selection, while others lead open groups. Some teach the Playback approach. Others utilize Playback as a technique in their professional settings.

Finally, having emphasized as much as I have the elevated stature of the NST leader, I must not leave the subject without also stressing that the leader is a fallible human being. In company life, the leader will not make good choices all the time, following that law of human creativity which states that in any group of people the source of spontaneity will shift from individual to individual. As I stated earlier, the good leader will know this basic truth of group process and have the humility to let it work. Under any circumstances, the best idea may come from someone else. Inevitably, the leader will become blocked. Such occasions may occur infrequently and be very dramatic when they do, requiring a highly visible abdication. In daily practice, however, a good leader will let go of and re-assume authority constantly, as the dynamic of group creativity flows. Such an exercise takes a true willingness not to know what will come next. It is this kind of leader-genuinely humble, genuinely spontaneous, genuinely alert to the contributions of others--that assumes the highest stature and authority.

## THE EGO OF THE ACTOR

By its very nature, theatre demands both unusual leadership authority and a willingness to be led into highly vulnerable areas. Thus it should not be surprising if the world of the theatre is attractive to narcissistic and submissive/dominant personalities. The hierarchical nature of the star system undoubtedly is another contributing factor to a climate which raises a few to the level of quasi-divine power and submerges many in a sea of anonymity. In the heady intensity of theatre production, moreover, it can be tempting to abandon self-discipline.

Whatever the explanation, the fact is that the theatre is known to be full of people with giant egos. At the same time, many persons initially drawn to the theatre are put off by the undisciplined and egomaniacal atmosphere they find there.[7]

[7] Of course, actors are not alone in these manifestations. Lasch argues that an “intense preoccupation with self...defines the moral climate of contemporary society."(page 25)

The nonscripted theatre has been an alternative for dis- enfranchised thespians. NST's improvisational style, its ap- preciation of an integrated Mood-Program process, and most importantly, its respect for ensemble have all contributed to a climate which has placed a particular value on “egolessness."[8]

[8] See Chaikin, 59; Durland, 12. Beck writes about the Living Theatre in comparison to Grotowski's company: that whereas Grotowski is “authoritarian and separates the individuals, our [com- pany] tries to be communal." (Beck, 70) Uta Hagen expresses her own strong opinion from the perspective of a respected actress and teacher in the literary theatre: “Another reason for the collapse of venture after venture is sloth and egomania." (Hagen, 19)

“Ego” is a widely used but not always clearly defined term.
In Freud's system, it referred to the conscious self mediating between the id and the superego. In clinical mental health parlance, it often refers to an individual lacking basic self-worth as well as coping skills. In vernacular usage, the term is most often used pejoratively, meaning an excessively confident or solipsistic personality.

We know that in order to be spontaneous we need to rid ourselves of inhibiting thoughts, many of which are “I”- thoughts. For many of us these are fearful or unconfident considerations: we fear looking stupid, seeming crazy or being rejected. It is equally dangerous to spontaneity to have the opposite kind of “I”-thoughts: “I know I'll be good;" “They'll love me."

Spontaneity demands a sort of selflessness before the act.
We must be brave enough to enter the moment with a mind free of all protective considerations. We must enter into a nameless relation to the task, forgetting, for a brief instant, both its “it-ness” and our “Thou-ness” in the flow of action.[9] If we constantly need to be the center of attention and are jumping in without thinking, we are not being spontaneous because we lack flexibility. We are acting from a certain habit. Our spontaneity training would involve developing the “holding back” or “remaining at the edge” role and working on *appropriate* “jumping in.”

[9] Eric Bentley's essay, “Theatre and Therapy” contains a discus- sion of spontaneity in the context of Martin Buber's “I-Thou” concept. (Bentley, 385-401)

In fact, questions of individual spontaneity quickly become questions before the group, for any extreme role inflexibility--whether it is immoderate boldness, shyness, or whatever--will eventually attract the group's negative attention. In this way an unconfident person can appear egotistic, since he or she will end up interfering with the group's forward development. The democratic imperative of spontaneity also discourages the focus remaining on any one individual for very long. This does not mean that talent cannot be valued, but someone who is funny should be able to joke at the right time and be serious at the right time. The *spontaneous* gifted comic should be able to give over the focus, knowing that he or she--no matter how great the gift-will not always be needed.

In Playback Theatre performances, such as the one tran- scribed in the appendix, the actors wear coordinated clothing that diminishes rather than accentuates their individual differences (including sexual). The process of actors assuming different roles and giving and taking focus is totally visible and, of course, becomes part of the drama. In the course of an evening one actor might be chosen often for many key roles and another not. The audience sees the ensemble dealing with circumstances of the social level. Questions arise. Will the actors' egos interfere? Will the “stars” shine while the unchosen ones look more and more unhappy? Or will all be able to maintain an even, positive presence despite the vagaries of choicemaking?

In our Playback practices, we all try to speak briefly and to the point so that everyone has time to speak. If anyone is silent, we ask specially for his or her thoughts. Even more important, we try to suspend our habits of judging each remark, fostering participation rather than contention.  We've learned over time that the withdrawn person is important and merits our most creative attention. This means leaving space for reluctant speakers.

What is the point of such solicitude? For one, attention to silence helps us establish environmental attunement. Even in discussion, we are searching for that regenerative presence that Paul Tillich calls “being-itself.” In spirit, this practice is not unlike what happens at Quaker meetings.

Secondly, it is ultimately efficient. If one person's mood sinks, the group enthusiasm is reduced, whereas if the group can raise that individual up again, the entire company will be washed in a creative current, and the Program will proceed.[10]

[10] Paulo Freire describes such integrative moments this way:“A ‘current’ is established among the participants, dynamic to the degree that the content of the codifications corresponds to the existential reality of the group.” (Freire, 47)

I want to say a bit more here about isolates. Moreno empha- sized in his group dynamics research that the creation of “stars”and “isolates”in groups was not the consequence of pathology, but a natural factor of group life. He found that in any group, at any moment, on any question, individuals were drawn towards some of their associates and turned off by others. In other words, depending on the circumstances, I might choose one of you to have a talk with, and another for a dance. Furthermore, in the dynamic flow of the spontaneous moment, the context changes frequently. If you say something particularly interesting, I might change my vote and pick you for the talk. But then if you add that you are in a terrible rush, I might change again, since I want time for leisurely discussion. Moreno discovered what he called the “sociodynamic law," which said that there was a kind of capitalistic impulse in social choice-making that led certain individuals to be overchosen (the stars) and others to be underchosen (the isolates).[11]

[11] Fox 1987, 20-36.

Now we get to the important part. It was not enough to recognize this fact of social life, Moreno claimed. It was also necessary to use it as a basis for redressive action.

Moreno was an associate of Buber's, and was motivated by a strong sense of ethical responsibility. A major goal of his work was directed at methods for integrating isolates--both by helping them become more spontaneous and by helping groups accept them.

Jerzy Grotowski seems intuitively aware of these ever- present lines of rejection and acceptance in group life, but his solution appears to be different from Moreno's: to cut the lines of interpersonal connection. At any rate, that is how I interpret his rule of silence in rehearsals and comments like what blocks an actor most are “his fellow actors and the director.” Viola Spolin shows a similar reluctance to work with group choice patterns in her insistence that “all exercises are done with teams chosen at random."[12] Far better, it seems to me, is to work *with* the social level rather than ignore it (and as I have stressed, in the nonscripted arena it is almost impossible to ignore it). That takes knowledge, for a start. It also demands a social philosophy opposed to that which often prevails in a competitive, product-oriented context, in which the slow and the weak are eliminated from the sphere of action.[13]

[12] Grotowski, 211; Spolin, 30.

[13] Moreno's sociometric techniques have often been disorted, so that a “revolutionary category of investigation" designed to integrate isolates is used by leaders to increase their own power. (Moreno, 124)

I maintain that the fluctuations of group life are not threats to accomplishing theatre. Although it is never easy, they can be utilized creatively. There will be times, for example, when it will be marvelously energizing to put two actors together who have a special creative spark with each other; there will also be times when it will be best, in the interest of fairness and role flexibility, not to put them together. At such a moment, there may be a short-term drop in standard. But the long-term payoff will be overall improved standards and a company life that continues to thrive.

So far in this discussion we have opposed the egotistical with collectivistic participation in company life. It would be a grave mistake to leave the topic without mentioning the positive side of self-interest, which we can call personal autonomy.

Let us look a bit further at the idea of a group, which I characterized at the start of the chapter as comprised of individuals in an intimate, ongoing relation with one another. Clearly, the *quality* of their relating is important, for group involvement can mean loss of respect for individual persons, and often does. We want relating that is not one-way(abandonment of self and fusion with a larger body, usually personified by the leader), but two-way, which at its best can be called “love." As described by Erich Fromm, love is capable both of giving us a feeling of “uniting with the world” and a sense of “integrity and individualism.” Thus we have an ideal that fulfills both our desire for community and for personhood. Furthermore, in the Western Judeo-Christian tradition, where the concept of individual conscience has been a fundamental force, a person acquires sanctity simply by virtue of membership in the human community. So we care for each other not only in order to maximize motivation and team spirit. We care for each other because each one of us is important in and of ourselves, and we respect each other's individual needs.

A community, Jean Vanier has said, is a group of individuals who yearn to bring each other forth. I like that definition. It fits my sense that a theatre company is a group of individuals who feel themselves a part of an ultimately *loving* whole.[14]

[14] In considering this question of individualism, there are also significant cultural and ethnic differences to take into account. The Norwegians or the New Zealanders, for example, have a very different attitude to group life than Americans. And the Japanese have yet a different perspective. Even though I am promoting a concept of concern for others and individual forbearance, it is not without due sensitivity to historical or national prerogatives. When I teach in these countries I often urge individuals to be more “egotisti- cal.”

We have now encountered another major framework for understanding nonscripted theatre--cooperation and auton- omy in company life. We can add it to the others discussed in this section: nonthinking and thinking, freedom and structure, safety and risk, democracy and authority, Mood and Program. And as with the other frames, it is by following the principles of spontaneity that a group will find the right balance of individuality and connection in order to thrive.

Once while teaching a Playback Theatre workshop in Japan,
I noticed something during a break: a woman hurried outside with a friend, weeping. Before we started again, I asked her what was wrong. She hesitated to say, only commenting that she was sorry to bring the group down. I recognized the cultural difference here from a country like the USA, where an unhappy individual might not hesitate to want to be seen and heard. But in Japan, where the group feeling is so strong, the woman felt ashamed to be upset. In spite of her reaction, I sensed that in this case, it might well be good for her and for the group to stop and listen to an individual. I asked her permission to do this, and when the break ended, I asked her publicly what was wrong. She hesitated greatly, but finally explained. An earlier moment in the group had triggered something for her, an unhappy memory about a time when she couldn't say No. Her story touched many others, especially the women, and led to a rich and deeply satisfying vein of exploration.

Another time, a man told the story in an American workshop setting of seeing his beloved grandfather for the last time as a young boy. The actors acted out the story. It was very serious. People were very moved. As conductor, I then recognized a young man to be the next teller. But before he could come to the chair, a woman spoke up, in tears, saying that her grandfather had just died that week and she could not get to the funeral. Her unexpressed grief poured out of her. The others listened with sympathy. When I finally turned to the young man, he demurred, saying that his story was in a different vein and would not be appropriate. I believed him, and yet at the same time I sensed something I could not at the time have put into words. I felt his individuality, expressed through his choice of story, was not at odds with the group spirit. What was his tale? He told about being in the woods the day before and seeing a deer. That was it. The actors portrayed the scene. The person playing the deer held such stillness in his body. The person playing the young man held such wonder in his. We were all transfixed. Even now, it is hard to explain. But this story was in some way an answer to the others, and after it was over, we felt lighter in our hearts.

The many moments of a successful balance of personhood and group love blend together until it is hard to select them out. So many performances and workshops have ended with a feeling of being brought forth, both as individuals and as a group.

There have been many moments of inadequate balance, however. Let me offer two examples from the experience of the original company.

We reached a point after about five years of experience when some of the company (including the leader) wanted to become more active. Projects were developed and grants obtained. Five individuals committed themselves to remain primarily available for Playback work, while the rest of the company, at that time numbering six others, had full-time jobs. What developed in the course of the following four years was a split between what came to be called the “core" group--for all intents and purposes, the professionals-and the rest, who remained steadfastly amateur. The needs, interests, and skill levels of the two subgroups became increasingly diverse. What was once a wonderful spirit of togetherness eroded, never totally to be recaptured.

After seven years, Gloria, who was in the “noncore" group, decided to leave.  In a meeting with me, she discussed her deci- sion. I felt she had solid reasons, and I accepted it. However, when she told the group at the next practice, there was strong resistance. Gloria was one of our elders, she had wisdom, she was an important figure in our group and in the larger com- munity. The company refused to let her go, persuading her to stay under the conviction that “we are all one family." I also allowed the group will to prevail, even though I sensed that Gloria's decision to leave was right. The outcome of my cowardice was a year's further involvement for Gloria, during which the pressures which made her unhappy only intensified, and we all felt diminished.

There are no easy answers when the goal is ensemble and personhood; work and mutuality of relations.

Erik Erikson, in his study of Gandhi, stresses the importance of the mutuality between Gandhi's message and his methods: he preached autonomy and peace, and he insisted on a respectful, nonviolent interaction with his opponents. It was this mutuality which gave Gandhi his awesome political and spiritual power.[15] When a group of actors not only function well on the fictive level, but enjoy being together on the social level, a very strong communication is made. As spectators we rejoice at true teamwork, at manifestations of genuine interper- sonal excitement and joy. The example of community we see on such occasions is as inspiring as the content of the production and is one of the main appeals of theatre.

[15] “We are somehow joined in a universal therapeutics com- mitted to the Hippocratic principle that one can test truth (or the healing power inherent in a sick situation) only by action which avoids harm-or better, by action which harmonizes mutuality and minimizes the harm caused by unilateral coercion and threat.” (Erikson, 247)

There was once a musician in our company who began to show up late and became increasingly dispirited. A tense period followed, during which we felt dragged down by her apathy but committed to keeping her part of the group. Eventually, she decided to quit. On her last night, she was able to sum up her difficulty: “Doing Playback is like giving gifts. But I don't want to give gifts. All I want is to do my music. You are all so involved with each other. You are so open with the audience. I don't care about that. All I care about is my music."

She had expressed the nub of it. For in fact, improvisation precedes art, and nonscripted music, dance, or drama cannot be held apart from the people involved in them. We face a choice. We can opt for individual artistic expression. Or we can choose this mixture of relating and expressing which blurs the lines of product and self, and which cannot be done well without an open heart.
