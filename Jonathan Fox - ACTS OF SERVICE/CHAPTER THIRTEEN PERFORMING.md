# CHAPTER THIRTEEN PERFORMING

In this chanter I wish to emphasize the importance of understanding the special characteristics of an NST performance and to advocate the benefit of performance, so long as the context is right.

After discussing oral compositional style in Section One, the use of structural “building blocks” in improvisational performing comes as no surprise. Improvisation, while it deals in “no structure,” can only proceed by means of a diligent adherence to structure. Each distinct NST form will have its own formal elements--i.e., the rules of much-played “games,” the rigorously practiced techniques of psychodrama, the *lazzi* of a clown, or the laboriously-built outsized puppet for an outdoor pageant, the sequence of Playback fluid sculptures, pairs, and scenes. And while the dialogue and scene order may not be fixed prior to performance, the structural elements themselves are necessary components, learned and rehearsed.

In the absence of a play, this performance structure helps the actors enter and exit from their roles. Nevertheless, the em- phasis in the NST on the oscillation between fictive and everyday can make these transitions a problem point. Schechner emphasizes the importance of what he calls “warm-up” and “cool-down” in the actors' process, but he does not stop there. He cites training, workshops, and rehearsals as being necessary prior to warm-up; and a stage called “aftermath”following the cool-down. The thrust of these distinctions is to make performance but one part of a long, elaborate process. Schechner observes that compared to other cultures, the modern Western tradition is weak in providing effective transition rituals.[1] Ironically, NST performers, having forsaken theatres with dressing rooms, sometimes find themselves particularly bereft of transitional spaces.

[1] Schechner I985,16,126.

In Playback Theatre we have always placed importance on the warm-up to a performance, usually meeting as a group one and one-half hours before the start of the show (in our spontaneity approach to all that we do, the warm-up is never the same, but it does include as vital elements setting-up equipment and changing clothes). More problematic has been the post-performance ritual, due primarily to our intimacy with the audience. Typically we perform with no curtain and no stage. Often we exit from the space at the end of a performance(although sometimes even this is not feasible), but we are never far from the rush of audience members reaching to make contact. There is often a kind of party atmosphere at the conclusion of a show, and actors, still in their performance trance, get caught up in it, too. Yet individual performers often don't feel festive and need support. An immediate blending into the community is the last thing they want. So we hold a brief, five or ten-minute meeting after performances. After all, we also value the highly-charged post-performance contact with audience members.

At first we used this time for feedback--i.e., for critical analysis of each others' performance. But these meetings didn't always work the way we thought they would. Those actors who felt inadequate did not need to be told so. Furthermore, I feel that actors' capacity to give and receive criticism right after a show is severely limited, since they have not adequately cooled- down and are still for the most part in their performance trance.

So we changed to a ritual of actors speaking freely about their own experience, positive or negative, while the rest of us simply listened. The idea here is that by stating a feeling, it can be at least somewhat released, and the actor get some closure.

Critical discussion can wait. This procedure is a definite improvement over the more analytical approach, but even so, it has felt barely adequate to the task.

We also can expect from our familiarity with oral composi- tion that the style of the improvisational performances them- selves will be aggregate rather than climactic. Unfortunately, audiences conditioned to plays often have the wrong expectations of NST.

A professional theatre consultant once checked out our work. At supper after the show, he was silent for a long time. Finally, he exclaimed, “There was no ten o'clock spot!” It seemed like a bizarre evaluation at first, but of course, he was putting his finger on a crucial distinctionbetween NST and literary theatre. The ten o'clock spot refers to the inclusion of a minor climax about three quarters of the way through the play, just when the audience's attention is liable to be flagging. Not only did we not meet this criterion of theatre art, but in the consultant's view we failed on many others. We did not even have a major climax. There was no carefully modulated plot. There were no intermissions around which to heighten suspense. To one accustomed to such carefully crafted high points, Playback Theatre seemed alien.

When the improvisational comedy duo Shields and Arnell played on Broadway, they put a man in the audience with a light switch to cue a blackout when he felt a skit had reached its best ending--thus a compromise: a  nonscripted performance, with endings which seemed to the audience like the neat, high-energy endings of scenes from plays.

Without the man with the blackout switch, Shields and Ar- nell, for all their skill, experience, and teamwork, would not be able to “hit" the perfect ending every time. Their endings would be variable. This variability is a basic part of the oral process, and concealing it is to deny something about its essential nature.

In Playback, we do not work for a ten o'clock spot. Sometimes the climaxes come, sometimes they do not. Inadequacy for us lies not in “missing”climaxes, but rather to feel we are failing when they do not appear. The presence of mental rigidity, of an unwillingness to let the variability of the moment have its play, will kill our spontaneity and create an experience that really is lacking in excitement and expansion.

## CATHARSIS

The question of catharsis in nonscripted theatre is closely connected to variability. We observed earlier that the non- scripted theatre movement has overall shied away from cathartic, deeply purgative emotional experience for a variety of reasons, one of them being a reaction to sentimentality in the literary theatre. We mentioned the raw savagery of Artaud, who particularly despised feelings wrapped in birthday boxes of gentility. If we bring emotion into the theatre, let us go all the way, he insisted, let us taste delirium. Let us create a circumstance from which there is no escape. Brecht, while also starting from a position critical of bourgeois theatre, arrived at a differentanswer. Decrying the “spellbinding”effect of the theatre of identification (i.e., the theatre of Aristotle), Brecht called instead for an “epic” theatre in which the audience should “not be made to feel emotions, but to *think*." Augusto Boal, greatly influenced by Brecht, puts it this way: “Brecht wants the theatrical spectacle to be the beginning of action; the equilibrium should be sought by transforming society, and not by purging the individual of his just demands and needs.”

Brecht's choice of terminology is interesting. His language is very close to Plato's, who, you will remember, condemned the *rhapsodes* who “submitted our memories to the spell of [their] incantation." Thus Brecht put himself squarely in the tradition of modern science--and the ascendancy of mind over the senses--when he denounced “spellbinding”in the theatre. In using the word “epic,” Brecht was obviously referring to the didactic, or ethical, qualities of the old epics; he wanted his plays to teach, to give precept for the people.[2]

[2] See Esslin, I48-152; Boal, 106.

For me, the essence of what Brecht argues against is epito- mized in the curtain calls of the literary theatre. This gesture of humility, the bow, designed to mark the liminal transition from the fictive to the everyday, is carefully choreographed at once to simulate spontaneity and to stimulate the greatest possible eminence of the performers. This is theatre which encourages audiences 'to be childishly transported even with the curtain lowered. As a spectator, I find such demands distasteful. At the end of a show I want to show my appreciation; I do not hesitate to applaud. But I do not want to give over a certain personal autonomy, or be asked to do so. In fact, I welcome those occasions which are so particularly thought-provoking or moving that the evening ends, not in rapturous noisemaking, but in silence.[3]

[3] For appreciations of the “theatre of silence” see Brook, 47; Chaikin, 97;Grotowski, 44. Moreno published a feuilleton called “Das Schweigen" (Silence) in Vienna in I915. (Fox I987)

Like Brecht, I favor an adult exchange between actors and audience rather than one which invites a kind of infantilisma theatre that does not try to seduce me from my thoughts at the moment of termination, but has the confidence and courage to respect them. Nevertheless, even though I am also devoted to the idea of theatre as social transformation, I find his intellectualized stand against catharsis just as limiting as the view of the traditionalists who insist on it. Boal's argument that catharsis in the Greek theatre was a means for the ruling elite to defuse the just anger of the hoi polloi is not convincing (rather it seems to me that the Greek plays, with their urgent question- ing of the deepest issues of Athenian society, aimed at a truly liminal condition).[4] For example, experiencing at the theatre
(or anywhere, for that matter) the positive “feeling" of community may do as much to bring about a more equitable society than a theatre of ideas. In this sense, feelings may be ultimately seen as “political.”

[4] Boal, 46-7. For a defense of Greek theatre along these lines see Goldhill.
 
Catharsis defined as a sort of mindlessness, and criticized on that account, smells of literary prejudice. As we have suggested in looking at preliterary theatre, mindlessness isO.K. Certainly, we need nonthinking as much as we need thinking.

From a public health point of view, many major diseases in the developed world are associated with stress, and it would seem that a prime function of catharsis is to reduce stress. We feel better after a good cry. Or a good laugh. Modemn psychol- ogy has substantiated what common sense tells us, and recently biology supports this thesis with findings which suggest that catharsis produces biochemical responses of a curative nature.[5]

[5] See Cousins and Moody.

So in a society which has lost the idea of trance, we go to our therapist (at least some of us do) for a psychological catharsis and subsequent revitalization. And if we watch movies instead, or go see a good show, we may also be doing good for ourselves. Of course, despite the trend of modernism away from expressive activity, we still partake in dramatic rituals of a cathartic nature which have been important since prehistoric times, centering on birth, marriage, death, and seasonal change. We are likely to experience deep feelings on such occasions, and feel, somehow, more whole as a result.

Our understanding of the variability of catharsis in im- provisation has led us to the important area of catharsis and personal autonomy. I advocate a theatre that neither promotes feelings without mind, nor mind without feelings--a considerable challenge in a society like ours, which undervalues the domain of what Howard Gardner calls “the personal intelligences.” We must have at our disposal a requisite degree of “emotional literacy” if we wish to find the courage to plumb the deepest human stories. Clearly the theatre of catharsis is escapism only at its most superficial, for a full confrontation of the human dilemma cannot occur without stirring profound emotion.[6]

[6] See Gardner, 237-276.

In the nonscripted theatre the path towards truth is never charted in advance. This means that there are times when deep feelings come and times when they do not. If we are free of lit- erary culture's needs for predictability, we will accept an absence of catharsis as well as a truly earth-shaking climax.

In the NST, it is only with such total open-mindedness, such full acceptance of the variability of the moment, that the truly magical can occur. For there are times when the dramatic event crystallizes in an experience in which existent and fictive levels meet. These are moments when in Brook's words “both sides--actors and audience--relax to the point where judgment and defenses melt into shared experience." This is the catharsis which heals society. On such occasions we are not “audience” and “actors” but witnesses to a communal act of affirmation.

## THE QUESTION OF PERFORMANCE

In the fluid context open to oral theatre, where the separation between audience and performers may not exist and where “workshop” may be prized over “production," the question, “Why perform?” deserves serious consideration.

The idea of performance excites passion. We seem either to yearn for it, or walk in terror of it. Clearly temperament is a factor, and Jung's distinction between the introvert and extro- vert personality can help explain our response to this question. Conditioning is also very important. How many of us suffered unhappy performing experiences as children and shy away from theatrical and even public behavior as adults? Many people struggle with performing: an inner compulsion pushes them forward, even though the path feels tangled and rough. What is this curious power that performance has over our imaginations? Why is it that we care so much?

I think the answer lies not in any connection between per- formance and entertainment, or even performance and the dramatic, but in the connection between performance and spontaneity. A performance is an act that calls attention to itself. To do something adequately, anything, whether singing a song or speaking at a business meeting, may be difficult enough. Yet both to do the thing *and* call attention to our doing it at the same time is doubly difficult. Yet such frame- making makes for certain creative possibilities; it energizes the moment, pointing to a fusion of action and meaning. As human beings, we know we are capable of creative intentionality even as we know how easily intentionality can kill our creativity. It is a high risk, high gain situation.

Performance can be defined as acting with awareness. Self- referential acts are performances. The extent to which there are witnesses intensifies the context and challenges the performer to communicate in such a manner that the witnesses achieve a congruent understanding. Thus Paolo Freire would have us not just be in the world, but be able to be in the world and at the same time be aware of our position. He would have us be actors in history, performers in the human story.[7]

[7] “Acquiring literacy does not mean memorizing sentences, words, or syllables--lifeless objects unconnected to an existential universe--but rather an attitude of creation and recreation, a self- transformation producing a stance of intervention in one's context.”(Freire, 48) The weakness of Schechner's definition of performance as essentially “restored behavior” is its failure to emphasize the creative aspect of the self-referential act. (Schechner 1985, 36)

What distinguishes theatrical from everyday performance is primarily the fictive element. In theatrical performance, per- former and witness share an understanding about the action that “this is pretend.”Without the acceptance of this paradox, the dramatic fiction breaks down, and we are left either with “nontheatre” or deceit.

Earlier, I mentioned the theatre company which was denied funding because the official auditor felt the actors had lost control. At issue here were questions of congruence and the fic- tive paradox. The arts council judged that this company was committing the unforgivable error of engaging in “nontheatre," while the performers insisted that they maintained at all times fictive awareness.

Thus when performing theatre, we must communicate the frame of pretend around our actions, and around that the frame of *intentionality*, an awesome and exciting task, the ultimate form of spontaneity test and a tease to the playful genius of our intelligence.

To regard performance as a complex exercise in frame- setting stimulates a number of reflections. Now we can appreciate even more the importance of beginnings and endings in the theatre, since a theatre performance by definition is an act with a double frame around it. Performance artists and other experimental theatre performers often create pieces where the lines are not clear between performing and non-performing as a way of drawing attention to the centrality of the question of framing in the theatrical experience. Of course, the effectiveness of these experiments will depend on the artist's level of awareness and ability to communicate that awareness-- in other words, how successfully the artist makes clear that the frame is about framing.

Performance--setting out to do something and calling attention to the fact-is exactly what the superstitious part of us wants to avoid. Thus if you buy a lottery ticket, whatever you do, don't talk about winning or you will jinx your chances, and if you are about to perform on stage, do not talk about succeeding. “Break a leg," we say in the English-speaking world. Or in the French: “Merde à la puissance treize.” Now these are fascinating communications, ostensibly having nothing to do with the subject at hand, except that they are so paradoxical. The thought that a performance is about to take place is conveyed by the speaker's superstitious effort to avoid the subject, in which an insult is, in fact, a message of good wishes. Thus the “insulter” is saying, “You are about to perform. It is a terrifying thing, which we must not refer to, or I will give you bad luck. So I won't refer to it, and this means, ‘Good luck.'"

Personal experience with nursery drama groups suggests that three year-olds are certainly capable of performance--i.e., self- referential fictive group play. Thus it would appear that we are able to perform from a remarkably young age.

In fact, the etymology of the basic dramatic terminology be- lies the widespread point of view that theatre is a nonessential: “play,” our word for a script, is the same as our word for freedom of movement and carefree enjoyment;“actor,”our word for a fictive role player, resembles our word for the very process of doing. The rich meaning of these words may also suggest their ancient place in the history of verbal expression and human consciousness.

We can also make use of our concept of performance to elucidate the idea of mental health. In his theory of the transitional object, Winnicott emphasizes the importance of the “me-not-me" paradoxof the capacity of a normal infant to believe in the aliveness of its teddy bear and yet know it is not real. “Playing," he writes, “is inherently exciting and precarious. This characteristic derives not from instinctual arousal but from the precariousness that belongs to the interplay in the child's mind of that which is subjective (near- hallucination) and that which is objectively perceived (actual, or shared reality).” Playing, Winnicott believes, leads to shared playing, which leads to cultural experience.[8]

[8] See Winnicott, 52.

Performance can be regarded as a sophisticated form of play--play with a metalevel frame calling attention to itself. Many three year-olds in the nursery school do it with ease, while others are already experiencing blocks to their emerging capacity for play and performance. They cannot pretend; they cannot play; they cannot be aware that they are doing these things; they can be aware but not to the extent of sharing their awareness with witnesses; or they cannot manage the transitions between performance and nonperformance.

The challenge of creative manipulation of such frames of play is just what drew GregoryBateson, an early cybernetician, into the field of psychology.A person who is not fully human and healthy cannot play, or is always playing, or cannot understand, or is always understanding, and most especially, does not move fluidly between the frames of action and awareness.JIn this sense the performer is a well-adjusted, always readjusting person, able to perform and not perform ap- propriately. Actors in the literary theatre have a reputation, sometimes maliciously ascribed, of “always performing;"the more it is true of a particular individual the less he or she is a true performer (or we might say, the more a neurotic performer). In the improvisational world of NST, the visibility of the process of entering and leaving the performance state makes it very difficult to be successful without being “a true performer.” This is perhaps why the NST seems to fail to engage the sustained interest of the histrionic, inflexible, and immature personality. In the literary theatre, the separation between personal and fictive personality enables actors who are neurotic performers to achieve success; the very absence of such separation in the NST makes the obstacles for the neurotic actor difficult indeed.

If we accept the role of performance as part of the “evolutionarystep” (Bateson's phrase) human beings are making towards increasingly complex and creative forms of action and awareness, then it is important to come to grips with the widespread reluctance of individuals to accept their heritage as homo agens (human beings as actors). We have already mentioned a number of possible factors: a cultural bias against the expressive, an endemic anxiety, and alienation.  In addition, settler conditions in the immense colonial countries required a fierce individualism that militated against such communal and unpragmatic activities as the performing arts. Thus an old-country tradition of native dramatic association may have atrophied, and modern mass society, with its passive entertainments, has starved it even further. Recently in Norway, at the end of a week-long workshop, a final celebration brought home this point to me. A committee had organized the event, which comprised skits, music, poetry reading, party games, and light-hearted rituals. The participation was so full, and the quality of the individual segments so spontaneous that I felt myself in the presence of a tradition of dramatic expression quite foreign to my American experience, where it is exceptional even to play charades.

Yet even beyond these reasons, performance is eschewed, for the reason alluded to at the start of this chapter. Many people (usually in childhood) have miseducative experiences in theatri- cal performances. It is for this reason, primarily, that many teachers in the educational, community, and therapeutic theatres are wary of performance. They do not want their charges to be hurt. Such concern suggests that they are living up to the redressiveresponsibility associated with NST activity.

We can examine performances which do harm from three points of view: the context, the_director, and the actor. The context can be unfortunate in many ways. For instance, the auditorium can be too large for young, untrained voices, so that the young actor comes away with the memory of a restless, dissatisfied audience; the play can be too difficult; the rehearsals too high-pressured. If the director mismanages any aspect of the job, especially if he/she turns authoritarian or overly judgmental, then an experience of theatre may turn into an instance of oppressive leadership. Even when the context is well chosen and the leader skilled and caring, the actor may just not be up to the test at hand. In such a case, certainly with young or disabled individuals, the leader bears significant responsibility for inviting them to participate in an experience which backfires for them.

To say that failure is inherent in the improvisational process is not to say that failure has no meaning. The frames of intentionality that define performance also de-limit successful performance. The evaluation will be subjective, and may vary among the participants (performers and witnesses), but usually there is a general sense of whether the performance is a success or a failure. The complexity of this evaluation does not diminish its importance.

We can castigate the directors who charge bullishly towards performance in the name of art. Nevertheless, in my view a position which steadfastly eschews performance is not any better.  Performance is a fundamental aspect of theatrical expression. As such, the question becomes, not whether or not to perform, but how to create the right performance context.

Here are some examples:

*Three-year-olds* in a thirty-minute drama class are asked to act out the story they have just heard. They are allowed to pick any role--i.e., no one is specially chosen or rejected (thus we may have six wolves, three Red Riding Hoods, one tree, and no grandmothers). The enactment will probably take place only once--whereas, with slightly older children, there might be a “rehearsal”and an “improved”version. Children who have difficulty with any aspect of the performance will be worked with individually (often at a later session).

*Seven-year-olds* coming to a Saturday morning Playback acting class are told that at the end of the series they will be invited to do a scene in a performance of the adult company. The challenge excites them, spurring them to greater application. A!l who wish to will be permitted to perform.

*Teens*, identified as being “at-risk," take part in a ten- session drama group. Their self-esteem is too low to permit any kind of public performance in the time allotted. So the teachers focus on helping them perform for each other, setting up low-key contexts--the performance segment may only last fifteen minutes-which suit their capacity for success.

*An adult Playback group* organizes to enact stories only for themselves. Performance, for them, becomes those moments of dramatizing each others' stories.

*Another Playback group* begins that way, but slowly out- grows its “nonperformance”scope. The group decides to hold monthly public performances, which causes them to focus on improving standards, theatricality, and other means of making successful communication. Their public image is very low-key, however, and congruent with their ambition. Thus there is no advertising and no financialpressure. The audience rarely exceeds twenty people and consists of individuals for the most part already allied to the concept of sharing personal stories. Thus the context is public, but sufficiently intimate to keep the pressure low.

The thrust of this chapter has been to advocate creative performance contexts. With our own Playback group, we performed frequently and very soon after starting up--with an appropriately low-key test, if you remember, in the church hall; as a result, actors originally fearful of shamanic energies transformed themselves, buttressed by confidence in their form and experience in creative risk-taking. Month by month, we learned to utilize the power of performance to lead the witnesses on an adventure. These were experiences during which the heart often cringed, and even sank, but at the end felt freed from care and often remarkably refreshed.
