# CHAPTER TEN THE IMPROVISATIONAL DIRECTOR 

It seems to me that there has been more cogent thinking about the relaionshih bewecen performes and the audience than performers and director. Thus Brecht was concerned to foster the “adulthood” of audience, and Augusto Boal, influenced by Paolo Freire as well as Brecht, wrote about the “active” involvement of the audience as a way of combatting the actor-provider, spectator-receiver idea. In this concept, dramatic communication is a “dialogue” between equally adult parties. This way of thinking ignores the essentially regressive aspect of the audience experience, but it does, at least, show respect for the capacity of theatregoers to respond in a mature, autonomous manner.[1]

[1] Boal dislikes the word “spectator,” and advocates a kind of “simultaneous dramaturgy." (Boal, 104, 132, 142, 154) See also Freire, 109. Paolo Freire has also written *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, alluded to in Boal's title. For a view which emphasizes the appropriateness of “passiveness” in an audience, see Cole, 73.

There is a similarity between the actor-audience experience and the director-actor experience. In both cases, the latter is led into uncharted territory by the former. This leadership role brings with it great power. In the case of actor-audience, the relationship is temporary. Thus no matter how malleable the audience becomes, or how magical the actors, the autonomy of the audience and the humanity of the actors is vouchsafed by the quick termination of the performing context. Outside it, the reverse is often true. Actors, on the fringe of economic society, are often perceived as the weak_ones, while the critics in the audience, the lawyers, parents, students, all secure in their social positions, are seen as being strong. However, with regard to the director-performer relationship, especially in the NST, it is common for the idea to implant itself that the director (auteur, teacher, administrator) is enthroned for the duration.

We have said about spontaneity that it demands creative responses to discrete moments of experience. Every human has the potential for spontaneity; every human can by conscious learning become more spontaneous. It is equally true that no individual, directors included, are perfectly spontaneous. When discussing leadership, then, we cannot help but focus on the question of spontaneity for an entire group and the place of leadership in fostering or restricting it. Spontaneous leadership, like individual spontaneity, is highly dynamic, involving frequent frame shifts and decisions for action. *These include giving over the leadership when a creative possibility comes from a nonleader group member.*[2]

[2] Moreno was deeply interested in the question of spontaneous leadership, and in fact his ideas on the subject influenced group studies in psychiatry and sociology. Moreno contended that in any group there was an internal structure as well as an official structure and that the most chosen individual(s) at a particular moment on a particular question was often at variance with institutional leadership. Thus he developed what he called a “revolutionary category of investigation,” designed to allow individual spontaneity to flourish in a group setting. See Fox 1987, 20-31.

Let me give two generic examples to illustrate the point.

It is time to start rehearsal, but Sara is late. In spite of the importance of a whole-group warm-up, the director decides to go ahead out of Program considerations: there is a lot of pressing work to do. Five minutes later Sara walks in; she is in tears. The leader and the group are then faced with a second focal moment. What is the best response to Sara? Let us say the director decides to continue working. It may be the right decision. But it may not be. What if the director feels so pressed by Program thoughts that he/she doesn't care about Sara, or is angry at her for complicating the process of getting down to work? It may be someone else in the group who knows the extent of her difficulty and reaches out to her. At this point the director can become even more impatient, for now not only is Sara late but Rachel has been distracted by her.

Alternatively, the director can temporarily let go of the  agenda, allowing Rachel, on behalf of everyone, to welcome and support Sara. Such a decision may require two minutes, or it may take ten. It may take an hour. If the Mood of the group is enhanced by stopping for Sara, then motivation for the Program will be high when work is resumed, and the authority of the director will be enhanced by virtue of his/her ability to pass the leadership over to a group member--in this case, Rachel.

In Playback the criterion for leadership will change from moment to moment. The director may be the chosen leader to accomplish the Program, but Rachel, wise, warm Rachel, may be the individual chosen by the group on the question of dealing with a personal crisis. This is a concept neither of rigid authority nor leaderlessness, but of a power which moves from person to person according to the needs of the moment.

Such fluidity requires humility on the part of the director and courage on the part of the performers, for any one of them may need to take over leadership at any time. It is a philosophy which demands a highly mutual and adult respect between group members, and will not work in a context where the leader craves veneration or the members crave a guru.[3]

[3] Actors are not alone in their proclivity for submission, as Becker has eloquently elucidated in *Denial of Death*: individuals in society as a whole exhibit a widespread tendency to give over autonomy to a higher force, whether it be their God, rock star, or baseball team. Becker sees this phenomenon as a sociological manifestation of the transference principle.

Another example: the director is about to begin a vigorous movement exercise when one of the group members sighs and says “God, it's hot." Spontaneously, the director lets go of his or her' supremacy, allowing the comment to transform the original idea for the warm-up in the direction of environmental attunement: So the director prefaces the instructions for the activity with: “It *is* hot, yes. Let's each take off a piece of clothing and..." In this case, if the director suggests taking off some clothing, and everyone laughs, gets animated, and the practice is off to a wonderful start, it will be because the director had the spontaneity to give in to Fred, who for a moment provided an opening for the group.

Of course, in both these cases, the spontaneous response from the director might well be to *not* indulge a member's intervention. Sara may always be late, or always upset. Rachel may always seek to escape work by a trumped-up concern for others; Fred may chronically complain as his way of resisting.

The above examples may seem trivial; nevertheless, they exemplify a continual challenge. The good leader will give over a thousand times, following the law of spontaneity, because creativity belongs to no one all the time, but wafts about us all.

And the good leader will know when his or her own spontaneity is blocked, and be willing to turn the leadership over then, too. Of course, from a psychological standpoint, it is precisely when we are blocked that we often are most blind, in which case it is appropriate for members to be assertive (which is perhaps what happened in Schechner's“boss”episode cited earlier, when despite his principles to the contrary, he became so authoritarian, his actors rebelled).[4] Again, it takes humility to say, as director, “I cannot do this; please, someone else lead us here." We are liable to fear that if we admit weakness, we will never direct again-or be allowed to. But the spontaneous concept of leadership can give us confidence to let go, for we know that a moment later, we will be asked to lead again, as indeed we will, *especially* if we show flexibility and self-knowledge in allowing the participation of others.

[4] See chapter 5, note 10.

## THE IMPORTANCE OF MOOD
This discussion on leadership has emphasized a fluid shifting of creative initiatives in directing group life. Implicit in this discussion of the director's process is also an assumption that he or she has the spontaneity to alternate effectively between Programmatic and Mood frames. While the literary theatre can purport to make a separation between personal and professional concerns, the line is never so clear in holistically- oriented NST such as Playback Theatre. Let us take some examples (all real) of bad directing:

*A director of some experience describes an improvisation exercise to a pair of student actors, gives them the signal to start, watches their performance, then shakes his head and tells them what they did wrong. The director gives a similar message to the next pair, and the next. The spirit in the room grows heavier and heavier, and the standard of execution falls as the criticisms mount.*

Here is a director who either did not care to modify his critical approach as his actors grew dispirited, or did not know how--i.e., did not have the communication and artistic skills to frame his suggestion in terms of a constructive improvement within his actors' grasp.

*A student director is leading a trust exercise, involving members of the class diving from a chair into the arms of the others. One actor is afraid to fall. He hesitates. The others cry “Jump, jump!" The director watches, silently, as the actor finally half-jumps, half-slides down to the floor.*

The purpose of the exercise, to increase trust among actors, will not be achieved for this actor, whose timidity has not been respected; he may have jumped only because he felt pressured by the enthusiasm of the crowd; at any rate, he did not really jump at all, and was undoubtedly left with a sense of failure. There was an opportunity for the director to intervene here, helping the actor find a way to successfully complete the task.

Instead, the director remained passive, providing guidance neither for the actor nor the rest of the group.

*A famous director is leading a similar exercise for community people as part of a one-time only “outreach" pro- gram. He tells the 30-odd participants to make a large circle and run across it with their eyes closed. One young woman is very afraid. When she hesitates, he exhorts her to run. She runs, or half runs, across the circle. There is laughter. The director calls for the next runner.*

It was insensitive to pressure the woman to run--actually, it was probably insensitive to conduct this exercise at all (for what was the purpose of these strangers building up trust with each other when they were never going to be together again?). And it was insensitive not to say anything redressive after her ordeal, during which she was treated as an object of fun.

*Another well-known director, known for his homoerotic sensibility, is conducting a week-long workshop. The male and female participants consist mostly of college theatre majors. Some of the students privately express fears about the homosexual and violent nature of the director's work. That the director's assistants are all male adds to the sense of tension. During the week, the work that the students are encouraged to create is indeed in the area of stepping beyond inhibitions regarding theatrical sex and violence. The director makes this possible by his warmth and wisdom, which he shares with everyone, women and men alike. One woman, however, who in her final piece plays a highly sexual love scene, becomes overwrought. Her private vulnerability to issues of love, passion, and manipulation is too great for her to be able to integrate the workshop activities. So on the final evening of the workshop she verbally attacks the director. A protracted argument ensues, involving a good half of the participants. The next day, the staff cancels the final group piece. No public mention is made of the argument of the evening before. In the interests of salvaging the positive aspects of the week, everyone papers over the awkwardness, and the workshop ends on a falsely happy note.*

It is inevitable that deep questions surface in such a workshop. The outburst of the woman may have been regret- table, perhaps, but as such it probably indicated a need for frank talk long before. the last evening. Once the lid was off, the issues most certainly should have been discussed directly. There was an opportunity here for the director/teacher to bring his students over a threshold, but he was unwilling to encounter them, either for fear of dealing with their reactions or because he himself was confused and did not dare appear so.

Each of these examples reflect instances when directors be- trayed an inability to integrate Mood concerns with their
Program. It is perhaps instances such as these which give theatre a reputation for being cruel, and lead so many people over the age of ten to be terrified of dramatic activity.

There are directors who appreciate the value of attention to
Mood but nevertheless bullishly stick to their Program because of an uneasiness about mood-oriented group process. The disclaimers of many directors regarding therapy may reflect such a discomfort. A prevalent way of looking at this issue is that Program process is art, while Mood process is therapy. If my definition of spontaneity has validity, then the NST director must be skillful in both spheres. Thus any NST director should acquire skills in Mood process leadership so that he or she can pay attention to someone in distress-or not pay attention-with confidence.

When I started my career in improvisational theatre, I knew that anything might come up, not only in rehearsals, but in performances as well. In fact, the whole Playback idea was based on the premise that any story was appropriate, and so the most traumatic and emotional stories might be voiced. I did not have a fear of the artistic challenges of dramatizing the spontaneous story, but I did fear the Mood side of things, on two counts. First, I was afraid of strong emotion and suspected I might unwittingly prevent the enactments from achieving their full power. Second, I feared that such strong moods might be unleashed that I would not know how to handle them (as conductor, I felt particularly responsible to guide audience and actors in and out of highly emotional moments). So I acquired psychological training, which included personal therapy as well as professional education. It gave me what I needed-the skill and confidence to be as sure-footed in the arena of Mood as I was in the arena of art, and I was able, much more than before, to adequately shift between the Mood and Program modes both in rehearsals and performances.

The examples above have emphasized occasions where directors did not know how to deal with Mood considerations. The opposite can just as easily take place, of course, although it is not so frequent. Directors whose Mood handling attentions overwhelm their Program interests will find themselves leading a discussion or therapy group rather than a theatre group. As leaders, they will constantly be giving over to Mood concerns, and little will be accomplished of practical nature.

Two actors in our company had been in a relationship with each other. But it was over, and Vince would soon marry someone outside the company. The interaction between Vince and Judy was awkward and it affected their work. The company decided to spend a day to help them clean things up.
It was a frankly therapeutic session. It was a painful day for us all, and it did not end in roses and champagne. How could it? For ultimately their story was one of unfulfilled dreams, compulsions, doubts-all mixed up with love.

That day had no direct bearing on actual theatre work, and yet I believe it helped us face the deep and difficult truths that we aspire to embody in our scene work. As such, it served Program needs. And the air was cleaner. Judy and Vince felt supported and validated rather than guilty and isolated. We were all able to work in a better spirit.[5]

[5] When we needed help with our group process and did not have the expertise or detachment to provide it ourselves, we engaged a facilitator.

There can be no recipe for which way to go, towards
Program or Mood. It always depends on the context. Being able to go either way, and knowing when, is a mark of a spontaneous director. 

## “NICE” DIRECTORS

We concluded in the previous chapter that risk-taking was essential for liminality, but that there was also such a thing as a bad risk in the theatre. Peter Brook has been quoted as valuing a working atmosphere of *danger* above all others, and he has written about Grotowski that the “tense climate" of his rehearsals makes “certain experiences possible.” Moreover Brook has said that “it is the modest director, the honorable, unassuming one, often the nicest man, who should be trusted least."[6] Here Brook is criticizing, I suspect, the safe, complacent approach which ignores the energy and creativity of human passion.

[6] Heilpern, 214; Brook 1968,125,108.

Actor and director have a compact. The actor suspends a layer of thoughtfulness; the director shoulders it, freeing the actor to go deeper into the sensory, intuitive world. The actor is movement, sound, and physical presence; the director is the eye, the judge, the outsider. The director sets the frame for the actor.

Entrusted with actors’safety as they execute a task that almost inevitably will cause them to spill over, lose the thread, become unmoored, directors have greatresponsibility. The actors will succeed if and only if the director can maintain the right frame of understanding and the right mood. On this level, I take exception to Brook and say that ultimately, directors must be nice--which is to say that if a director's severity is couched in loveit can be withstood, but if a director's severity is rooted in bitterness, misanthropy, or greed then the actor may well find him or herself on dangerous ground, and the question of trust becomes an appropriate, if unfortunate, concern.

Theatre utilizes human beings for its operation. Western ethical practice insists on the sanctity of the human condition. Therefore there can be no question. Theatre must be for the good of the actors.

An interviewer once asked Ingmar Bergman if he had ever chosen an aesthetically inferior solution for non-aesthetic reasons. Bergman answered, “No.” In the NST, where art and therapy are twinned, such dogmatism will not wash. I don't know about Brook or Bergman. I have only their words and the words of others-in the stark, disembodied form of print on a page-to guide me. I hope they would not sacrifice me to their principles, if I was one of their actors.[7]

[7] Bjorkman, Manns & Sims, 130. That both Brook and Bergman attract loyal actors, who work with them for years, is a positive sign.

Finally, let us end with respect for the courage of directors who are willing to take responsibility for knowing so that actors can let go. In this discussion of the director's role, we have discussed the importance of giving leadership away, developing sensitivity to Mood, and maintaining an open heart. It is out of such humility and caring that directors will be granted the deepest authority for the realization of their dramatic vision, based on mature permission-giving from the human subjects themselves.
