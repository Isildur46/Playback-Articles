CHAPTER TWO THE LITERARY STYLE

Now let us look at the characteristics of literary expression-remaining for now in ancient Greece.

Homer lived, it is surmised, in the ninth century B.C. By the fifth century, when Athenian culture was at its height, the literary mind had become a driving force, but it was not as yet entrenched.

Socrates'trial can be seen as a desperate victory for an old guard of die-hard oralists; what was still unacceptable, and what Socrates had dared, was to express truths in terms of ideas, not stories, and to embody his spiritual calling in an abstract “voice” that paid no homage to the traditional pantheon of Hellenistic deities.

Plato, Socrates’ beloved student, attacked the oral mode from a different vantage point. In the *Republic*, he insisted that “we can admit no poetry,” for poetry “waters and fosters feelings [of sex and anger] when we ought to dry them up, and it establishes them as our rulers when they ought to be ruled."[1] For the rhapsodes, the performing poets, certainly stirred up feelings, inducing a kind of trance as they passed in their recitations from event to event, from “experience to experience, submitting our memories to the spell of the incantation.” Plato scholar Eric Havelock surmises what it is like to be a listener:

[1] Hamilton, 832.

    The whole experience becomes a kind of dream, in which image succeeds image automatically without conscious control on our part, without a pause to reflect, to rearrange or to generalize, and without a chance to ask a question or raise a doubt, for this would at once interrupt and endanger the chain of association.[2]

The very sensuousness inherent in the auditory narrative performance needs to be denied if we are to learn how to “think." Plato and his school were polemicists for thought over feeling, for philosophy over story, for argument over enchantment. “The worst of *authors*,” wrote Plato, making a dig at the bard's loquacity, “will say something which is to the point."[3] That the philosophers had to work so hard to prove their points is testament to how entrenched were oral habits. Nevertheless, the Greek achievement was to give birth to modern literary culture--dooming the oral way to a long eclipse.

[2] Havelock, 190.
[3] From The Phraedrus, in Plato, 273.

Aristotle, Plato's student, also contributed to the argument.
His *Poetics* is significantly a tract praising a literary work, the tragic play, at the expense of the orally composed epic poem. Aristotle likes the conciseness of tragedy's plot structure, compared with the epic, which is “spread over a long time and so diluted.” The epic genre also contains an unfortunate “multiplicity of plots,” while tragedy compresses and is selective. “What, for example,” says Aristotle, “would be the effect of the *Oedipus* of Sophocles if it were cast in form as long as the *Iliad?*"

The value placed by Aristotle on brevity, unity of action, and climactic structure is a direct attack on the aggregative oral style. It is no surprise that Aristotle ranks tragedy above the epic, sealing the matter with the point that tragedy, unlike epic, “has vividness of impression in reading as well as in representation.”[4]

[4] Aristotle, 117.

According to Aristotle, then, literarystyle includes the following qualities:

*Conciseness*. The hypnotic conjunctions and oft-repeated phrases which are a stylistic necessity in oral composition lose much of their function on the written page. Moreover, while the ear may relish the sounds of these words, the eye chafes at their repetition. Additionally, the lists, catalogues, genealogies, rituals, and other descriptive material which are essential to the epic if it is to fulfill its purpose of cultural storehouse,  lose much of their importance in print.

In school, we are taught to become true inheritors of our literary culture; teachers drill us not to repeat ourselves, for the thrust of literary composition is in the opposite direction, towards “unwasted” words and abstract terms. As far as syntax is concerned, we are taught a subordinate, rather than an aggregate style.Thus, writing leans towards maximum brevity, and our conditioned response is to find distasteful a play that lasts more than two hours (some Bali dramas take all night), a speech of more than one-half hour (in developing countries, political speeches may take three to five hours), or journalism requiring more than fifteen minutes to read.

*Abstraction and thought*. Although research is far from conclusive on this question, it appears that writing and thinking are concomitant phenomena. To conceive of symbols as representing linguistic sounds is in itself so significant an achievement, according to Ong, that in the cultures of the world, the alphabet, as distinct from a script (Linear B), pictographic system (Chinese), or syllabary (the Japanese Katakana syllabary), was invented only once, among Semitic people about 1500 B.C. All modern alphabets derive from this invention.

Once the alphabet came into being, the process towards the manipulation of the symbols and the concepts they stood for continued with increasing sophistication. And it continues.[5]

[5] See Ong, 37ff.

The language of *Oedipus*, for example, compared with Homer's, illustrates both the distillation and the thoughtfulness of the literary style. In the opening scene, which gets right to the presentation of the hero (in the *Odyssey* over two thousand lines precede the hero's appearance), Priest's complaint and King's reply are pregnant with ironical metaphor that is quite foreign to the concrete style of the epic: Homer's storm-tossed sea meant literally bad weather, but the Priest's“Thebes is tossed on a murdering sea" is metaphor, and Oedipus's gesture of *noblesse oblige* (“Sick as you are, not one is as sick as I") foretells his terrible fate with a literary prescience.

To say that oral culture expresses itself in concrete terms is/ not to say that writing is never concrete. But along with writing came additional possibilities of abstraction which vastly increased the scope of human expression--in vocabulary, syntax, and content.

*Originality*. The means for preserving culture undergoes a` profound shift once a culture acquires literacy. Stability of the text renders obsolete the importance of stability of story (the “tradition"). The mnemonic imperative which required a metrical line dissolves. Any kind of line is possible, and any kind of “style.” Moreover, the author does not have the storyteller's responsibility to express value, for what the community needs to remember is available in other records. Thus the “storytellers” of literary culture have the splendid and often terrifying freedom of choosing what to set down. In the value system of literacy, they are rewarded for work that is new rather than traditional. The specter of forgetting no longer imposes its paradoxical rule of simultaneous conserving and recreating. Thus Sophocles had no responsibility to tell the whole story. It was remembered elsewhere. He could take a minor tale in Homer about a king who slept with his mother and expand it without fear of destroying a vital filament of remembrance. He could give the story his own original treatment, infusing it with personally vibrant thoughts about the nature of taboo, truth, power, and salvation.

*Distaste for the dramatic*, Insofar as literary culture devalues the spellbinding effect of tale-telling in favor of a less emotional approach more conducive to thought, it is not surprising that such a culture would disapprove of the idea of performance. Literary culture wants clear, cogent exposition, not “rhapsodies." Even more important, once there are records to refer to, then performance is not necessary anymore in the same way. Instead of a bard, we have a library to be a storehouse of knowledge and precept.

What I am talking about here is cultural value. If we take education as an illustration, we can observe an ineluctable tendency in the literary period away from a dramatic, interactive process of learning. Students no longer chant as they once did, or read out loud. Instead silent reading and silent thinking is the goal. We require children, full of vigor and energy, to sit placidly at desks, in order to encourage them to rise above their passions and think.

We can contrast the modern attitude of society  towards expressive activity to that of preliterary culture. As members of literary society, we learn a subject by “reading," or studying it. In contrast, “American Indians,” asserts Jamake Highwater, “look at reality in a way that makes it possible for them to know something by temporarily turning into it.” Furthermore, “Almost every aspect of Indian life has its counterpart in dancing and singing and is associated with ritual." Anthropologist Victor Turner corroborates the point with the remark that in pre-industrial societies, acting in life and ritual is essentially the same. Obviously, this is not the case in our own modern culture, where “acting” in life is associated with “acting out," and theatrical acting is deemed by many a frivolous pur- suit.[6]

[6] Highwater, 337; Turner, II5.

The literary theatre is a special exception to this general rule. Nevertheless, we can simply note, to stay with our example, how wordy *Oedipus* is (despite its conciseness), how it requires actors to declaim at length without significant action. Furthermore, since the work was not composed *in* performance, but simply *for* it, we do not need a performance to experience it (in fact, most of us have read *Oedipus* rather than seen it performed).

OBJECTIFICATION AND SPECIALIZATION

The literary mode, with its capacity for objective analysis, made possible great achievements in mathematics, science, and philosophy, but there was a cost.

Ong writes:
    In a primary oral culture, where the word has its existence only in sound, with no reference whatsoever to any visually perceptible text, and no awareness of even the possibility of such a text, the phenomenology of sound enters deeply into human beings' feel for existence, as processed by the spoken word. For the way in which the word is experienced is always momentous in psychic life.[7]

[7] Ong, 73.

In contrast, Ong asserts, the visuality of reading has an
“emptiness” which is devoid of the “interiorizing” nature of the heard word. Paolo Freire, the educator and expert on teaching literacy, makes the same point when he refers to sentences, words and syllables as “lifeless objects unconnected to an existential universe." The separation of the word from the human expression of it appears to have had among the most far-reaching consequences of all the effects of literacy. It has led one modern storyteller to describe his craft as “a possibility lost."

Marshall McLuhan also developed this theme, taking as his starting point the effect on culture of radical changes in communication technology. McLuhan divided history into three stages: oral, print, and electronic. He dated his era of print culture from the discovery of the printing press; hence the title of his major book, *The Gutenberg Galaxy*. (Our discussion so far suggests that the technological achievement of a written alphabet is surely as momentous as the printing press).

McLuhan emphasized the disassociation brought about by literacy, and his works provide many examples of its effect on Western culture. “The print-made split between hand and heart is the trauma which affects Europe from Machiavelli until the present," he wrote.[8]

[8] McLuhan, 170.

The deep interest of European culture in new methods, a trait not necessarily shared by other regions of the world, may explain the love affair of Europe and her colonial countries with innovative communication. Oriental culture, for example, has not made the same headlong leap into literary values as the West. Traditional forms, such as the Japanese tea ceremony, retain their impact in the face of modernity, and the oral concept of “practice” is appreciated along with the literary concept of “art."[9]

[9] Fernand Braudel, the eminent French historian, offers an intriguing explanation. Europe, he observes, developed very early a penchant for change as a value in itself, and in this respect was unique among the regions of the world.

Ong tells us that a primary oral culture uses about three thousand words for all its needs of communication and knowledge.Thus even the very units of expression are multi-purpose, unspecialized, and dependent for their meaning on the context of human interchange. The English language now includes about a million and a half words. We do not know them all, nor do we need to, for we can look them up, or consult a specialist--not in person, most likely, but through his or her *writings*.

Literacy made the inclusiveness of the oral story not only unnecessary, but even undesirable. Thus“subject”areas emerged, following the achievements of philosophers in “classifying” knowledge (what Aristotle was up to in the *Poetics*). Once knowledge itself increased beyond the capacity of any one individual to be knowledgeable about it, specializations developed, along with specialists, who assumed authority only for their chosen domain.

One of the specialized fields of endeavor which arose in literary culture was “art." By now it will be obvious that the oral bard did not conceive of himself as an artist in any sense that we know the word. “Artist” implies a self-conscious act of creativity which the storyteller would undoubtedly find discomfiting, for his efforts were bent on preserving the tradition. “Art,” furthermore,implies considerations which would seem at once both limiting and broad to the storyteller: too limiting in its reluctance to go beyond its domain into areas of providing information or therapeutic guidance, too broad in its freedom to choose style and content. [10]

[10] William Rubin notes, in his essay on the influence of tribal artifacts on European modern art, that “Africans have no word for ‘art,' although they are most certainly 'artistic'"(pages 27, 78n., 80).

Eventually the culture of specialization produced an idea of professionalism unknown in the preliterary world. Lord observed about Croatian oral culture that one became a storyteller in response to a calling and did not expect to live from practicing it (this is not to say that the standards of the “amateur” singer were not high). In fact, the great singers came from all walks of life, rich and poor, and all had other occupations.[11]

[11] Lord, 20.

Summing up, then, literary culture champions succinct, thoughtful, original treatments; it discourages the mesmerizing effect of performance; it champions objectivity--intellectual and aesthetic distance; it favors the specialist and the professional.

CULTURAL DICHOTOMY

We will be returning to many of these topics when discussing the contemporary practice of nonscripted theatre. However, before leaving this discussion of the difference between oral and literary modes of expression, I have some final observations.

The oral-literary dichotomy conforms in a general way to distinctions made by anthropologists between the thought processes in primal and advanced societies. Goody, for example, in *Domestication of the Savage Mind*, says that primal cultures value the “science of concrete,” “mythical and magical thought," and “intuition/imagination/perception," while modern cultures value “scientific thought and knowledge” and “abstract thought." Thus these distinctions seem to have an evolutionary aspect to them, although it has been vigorously argued that the supplanting of agrarian-based, nonaggressive societies, such as Crete, by imperialistic and more modern peoples, was not a mark of progress.[12]

[12] For the basic dichotomy, see Goody, 147. Raina Eisler argues that the accepted Western-scholarly concept of civilization, associated with the development of literary skills, has also favored a male-oriented “dominator” perspective and slighted the features of more female-oriented, "partnership" societies.

Minoan civilization had writing, but it was rudimentary compared to the flowering of literature of the Achaeans. And it is Athens we revere, not Knossos. Without doubt, there has been a strong feeling of superiority on the part of the developed nations towards oral cultures. “The great chasm that Western civilization has built between itself and ‘primitive' people is part of the complex mythology of the West, confirming its racist assumption of its superiority and its divine right to prevail no matter what the loss in customs, rights, and survival of others,” writes Highwater, a contemporary Native American scholar.[13] Many people today, at least intellectually, understand Lévi-Strauss's concept of the “totemic illusion,” the attitude that primal ways of thinking are not “still in us and related to our times." Yet the problem remains: primal practice continues to be the object of rapacity, ridicule, and gross misunderstanding.

[13] Highwater, 38.

A telling example is discussed by anthropologist Dell Hymes in his book *In Vain I Tried to Tell You*, which is really a kind of confession of biased scholarship. His main point is that in anthropological studies using Native Americans as informants, material was approached according to a literary bias that totally misperceived its content. For example, Franz Boas, a founder of the science of anthropology, made extensive use of North American materials in his scholarship, but transliterated as prose orally-composed verse of the kind we have been discussing. Moreover, he and those after him, including Hymes in the early part of his career, did not understand the vital role of drama and improvisation in the transmission of the material:

    The central theme of this paper has been the distinction between knowledge what and knowledge how, or more fully, between assumption of responsibility for knowledge of tradition and assumption of responsibility for performance. Much that has been published, I think, has neglected or confused this difference, treating tradition as something known independent of its existence as something done.[14]

[14] Hymes, 132.

In other words, there is no such thing in oral culture as a text without a performance. There is no such thing as communication as product without including the process of its transmission. Professor Hymes's acknowledgment was not written until 1981.

Once having made the oral-literary distinction, we must also be aware that the line is rarely stark between the two modes, both conceptually and historically. Sylvia Scribner and Michael Cole,researching this question among the Vai in Liberia, have found such a complexity of response to literacy that they prefer to call it a “practice” rather than a “skill,” concluding that“on no tasklogic, abstraction, memory,communication--did we find all nonliterates performing at lower levels than all liter- ates."[15] The historical perspective confirms the finding that despite its momentous effects, adoption of the literary mind-set is an incremental process. Manuscripts of Homer and Sophocles's works collected in the great library at Alexandria, for example, did not divide lines metrically nor distinguish between speakers, as they would today. The concept of alphabetical order was a late Roman innovation and word division in manuscripts was not instituted until the Middle Ages.[16]

[15] Scribner & Cole, 251.
[16] See Reynolds & Wilson.

Another point about our “great divide:” for some time, a process of reevaluation of the superiority of rational thinking over subjective, intuitive approaches has been underway in other fields besides anthropology. For example, Martin Buber praised an “I-Thou”over an “I-It” posture towards the world; C. P. Snow, in his book about the “two cultures," the humanities over science; Clifford Geertz,“ludic” over “mechanistic” paradigms for social analysis; Oliver Sacks,“iconic” over "computational” modes of perception.[17]

[17] See References for relevant citations.

Let us end the chapter by noting that if the playwrights of classical Greece have come down to us as the founding fathers of literary theatre, there has been a movement on the part of some theatre scholars to reevaluate their contribution. Thus Richard Schechner is very critical of the Dionysian Festivals in which the great tragedies and comedies which were performed, because “competition was preferred over aesthetics"--institutionalization, in his view, of the exclusivity pervasive in the literary theatre to this day. Augusto Boal, a South American director for a long time exiled in Europe, criticizes the “coercive system” of the officially-supported Athenian play contests as class imperialism, a situation which, he argues, did not pertain in the period before plays, when dithyrambic songs were staged “as a celebration in which all could participate freely.” Finally Richard Southern, unimpressed by the extant texts, refuses even to discuss the theatre of Sophocles and his colleagues on the grounds that there exists too little information about how the plays were produced.[18]
