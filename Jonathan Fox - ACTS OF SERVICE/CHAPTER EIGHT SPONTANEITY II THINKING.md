CHAPTER EIGHT SPONTANEITY II: THINKING

There is more to the subject of spontaneity than the challenge of “not thinking,” for the “as if” which forms the basis of theatrical action demands mental discrimination. Both performers and audience must know that what is being portrayed is “not real,” and know that they know. I was once familiar with an experimental theatre company, known for their portrayal of violence, which was turned down for an arts council grant because the auditor felt the actors had lost control. The performers were bitterly angry because they insisted they had maintained their understanding that “this was play." They felt that their very skill, in fact, had doomed them in the eyes of this clumsy-minded official. Was the auditor so fearful of what was portrayed that he lost his ability to discriminate? Or had the actors, indeed, got carried away and lost theirs? Clearly, in this case the understanding of what was “as if” was not shared, and the theatrical convention broke down.

Gregory Bateson, in his studies of animal communication, took particular interest in play. He found that play-acting was a means of conveying information as well as a vehicle for survival skill learning (cubs fighting with each other). Thus two dogs who initiate contact with mock snarls which then shift to friendly sniffing are saying, in Bateson's words, “the opposite of what they mean in order to get across the proposition that they mean the opposite of what they say."

Bateson distinguished between three types of messages among animals: a.) “mood-signs,” which are basic expressions of condition (hostility);b.) messages which *simulate* mood signs(mock-hostility); and c.) the ability to tell the difference-“messages which enable the receiver to discriminate between mood-signs and those other signs which resemble them. The message ‘this is play' is of the third type.”

Bateson argued that the third type of communication was of a different order than the first two. It was “meta-communicative." It defined the set of messages to which it referred--that is, it created a “psychological frame” of meaning around a particular phenomenon. Thus the two snarling male dogs both have an understanding that their threats are pretend, and this awareness enables them to be openly friendly because they already have communicated, in one paradoxical message, both their disinterest in being hostile *and* their readiness for combat if good relations sour.

Bateson's research led him to study schizophrenics. It would seem that the schizophrenic lacks a certain mental flexibility which prevents the achievement of metalevel understanding. Bateson writes about a golf game with a patient, which went fine until he suggested they keep score, at which point the patient broke down. The patient could comprehend the idea of “play,” but when the element of competition was introduced, he could not find metalevel integration for conflicting messages of “play” and “war,” “cooperation” and“competition,” “togetherness" and “self-interest."

These considerations lead us to another perception of spontaneity. Spontaneity is not only “not thinking.” Even though “not thinking” is involved in order to receive basic sensory and affective inputs, spontaneity also involves thinking of the highest order, where the nonrational and rational are comprehended in an understanding which surpasses the limitations of each.

Looked at from this viewpoint, someone who only commu- nicates “mood-signs," whom we might call childish, and an individual compulsively attached to rationality, whom we might call brilliant, may be equally unspontaneous.

“The difference between mad people and sane people," says
Brave Orchid, a character of Maxine Hong Kingston's *The
Woman Warrior* “is that sane people have variety when they talk-story. Mad people only have one story that they talk over and over.” Spontaneous persons have variety. They make adjustments to each new moment-appropriate adjustments. They can adapt.

When we are in the midst of a performance of Playback
Theatre, we constantly face the challenge of what to do next.
To be sure, we have our rough plan of a mixture of fluid sculptures, scenes, and pairs, but at each juncture we face the decision of what to do next: Act out another story? Play some music? Invite comment from the audience? Do something we have never done before? Wrap it up and go home? If we make the right decision--no, that is too mundane a way to describe it-if we can make the creative leap I am talking about, in which we both *experience* and understand the moment, then we have the chance to construct a truly spontaneous experience, a point of transcendence in which audience and actors are joined in their sense of involvement and purpose.

Such spontaneity is an integrating force, and provides a sense of culture as *connecting*. “There are bridges between the one sort of thought and the other,” Bateson says, “and it seems to me that the artists and poets are specifically concerned with these bridges. It is not that art is the expression of the unconscious but rather that it is concerned with the relation between the levels of communication."[1]

[1] See Bateson, 140-I, 188-9, 464. The account of Bateson playing golf with a mental patient can be found in Lipset, 217.

THE MOOD/PROGRAM FRAME

In this and the next two chapters, we will be looking at a number of conceptualframes which are of particular importance in NST production. By “frame” I mean a category of understanding, a principal theme of our story-of-the-moment, an appreciation of which will guide decisions for action. Since metalevel understanding is in constant oscillation, it is useful to think of frames in pairs, almost as if one were inside (or outside) the other. For example, a pair of frames for our discussion in the last chapter might be “not-thinking”and “thinking,” or “abandonment”and “control.” A pair of frames for the discussion of the performance text in chapter 4 was “the story of the moment is fictive”and “the story of the moment is on the level of social reality.” At any moment, an accurate sense of which polarity defines our experience is necessary for true spontaneity (appropriate behavior).

The first set of frames I wish to highlight can be termed
Mood and Program. To begin with an illustration, the Kung hold their healing dances about once a month. They know, however, that for their ritual (Program) to work, the atmosphere (Mood) must be right. If for any reason the community is fragmented or dispirited, they postpone the dance. Giving themselves the flexibility to allow their frame to change from“dance” to “no dance," they honor a principle of Mood/Program integration. In the modern professional world, on the other hand, such loose scheduling is unheard of. The Mood is not considered of sufficient importance to affect the Program (and dull meetings are common).

We noted in discussing Lord's work with storytellers that the tale, unfixed by the confines of text, could be modified, and often was, by a bard's spontaneous response to special conditions. If a distinguished person was present whom the storyteller wished to honor, he could easily add a phrase or two that pointed to the guest; if attention wavered, he could shorten the tale. The mark of a good bard, I suspect, was as much flexibility in adjusting the Program to the Mood as it was to have a far-reaching memory.

This is where the idea of performance is so important in the oral tradition. For each moment presents a new challenge, to be met with the heightened creativity we associate with a moment on stage.

Let me cite another example. Barbara Myerhoff, in her book about elderly immigrant Jews in a California community center,
*Number Our Days*, tells of the election and eventual removal of a leader, Kominsky, who surprised everyone by insisting on efficiency, order, and tasks. “He wished to effect a radical separation of personal elements, on the one hand, and ‘doing business' on the other." But the Center folk refused to diminish their attention to the social context, and eventually demanded his resignation. Kominsky was trying to be modern (Program- oriented) in a distinctly unmodem (Mood-oriented)situation.

Actually, the distinction between Mood and Program corresponds to the oral-literary dichotomy described in chapter 2. Thus *program* implies a stance which is logical, instrumental, paradigmatic, computational, while *mood* implies the ludic, the expressive, the relational. Our understanding of spontaneity suggests that in fact we must espouse not one or the other approach, but achieve the integration of both.(Of course, if society places a special value on Program, then this task of balance is difficult, for one must overcompensate, or appear to, in order to acquire the Mood skills we were never taught.)

Barbara Myerhoff makes another interesting observation in her study of the Aliyah Center: the women were much better adapted than the men. Her analysis is that the women, raised to be nurturing, pragmatic, outgoing, and attuned to their physical limitations were in a better position to be spontaneous than the men, who, conditioned to work and value abstract reflection, often found themselves unhappy in retirement. There have always been more women than men in our Playback Theatre activities. It seems women are closer to the expressive values of our kind of drama, while men, indoctrinated by a harder credo, hold themselves aloof. They begin farther away from a position of Mood/Program integration.[2]

[2] Myerhoff, 141, 244-5. On the subject of women in the NST, Sweet reports a different finding: The Second City companies had more men than women “because women were more restricted in the roles they could play in society.” (page 108) Another explanation is that since the comic-satiric theatre relies heavily for its effects on connecting with the dominant cultural frames, men, who are more associated with these frames, would be more successful than women, who remain an underclass. To this extent, the world of comedy is still primarily a male domain. In this connection, the presence of women in the experimental theatre movement has also been less visible than that of men, and feminist theatre has met with particular struggles to find critical attention, publicity, and support. See Chinoy & Jenkins, 277).

STRUCTURE AND FREEDOM

Keeping in mind the objective of Mood/Program integration as a pathway to spontaneity, I would now like to turn to a second area of special concern in the NST: the question of structure and freedom. In considering“improvisation," there is a definite connotation of second best associated with the word, according to which one is distinctly at a disadvantage when one has to improvise, be it a poem or a solution. At best, the outcome will conform to Moreno's dictum, of performing a new task adequately. But there is no promise of genius, no potential for transformation.

In the theatre, as I have mentioned, improvisation has been considered useful as a preparatory technique for acting that is not extemporaneous. It is used in actors' training, and in finding the subtext of a script. But it is not customarily utilized in performance, except at times of crisis, when something has gone wrong and an actor must “ad lib."

What is it about this liberty to invent which is so embarrassing? There are two explanations, both connected to a literary epoch's appreciation for the polished product. The first involves attachment to the true blessings of art and thought: an extemporaneous play is not very likely to mean much. The plot won't add up, there will be loose ends, the characterizations will be thin. Nor is it likely to *look* very good-no chance here for breathtaking costumes, backdrops, or lighting effects.

On the other hand, our penchant for carefully programmed and guaranteed results may find its origin in fear, for what may come when we have shuffled off our coils of thought may be chaos and confusion. Contemporary culture discourages the experience of the unknown with which improvisation confronts us. Freud's philosophy, based as it is on sublimation and repression, may ultimately be but an elaborate justification for our timidity.

The genius of Peter Brook has been that he has maintained a keen appreciation for both sides of this question--that is, a devotion to making, on the one hand, the best possible literary theatre, and on the other, to arduous experiments aimed at keeping actors alive to the unplanned moment. One such test, for example, consisted of a month-long trip through villages in central Africa with a troupe of actors who not only performed improvisations, but performed improvisationally. It was an extraordinary endeavor. Brook sought (his words echo Dorothy Heathcote) “a genuine beginning from zero.” The group had a minimum of effects, both for living and performing. They used no more than a rug for a stage, some wooden boxes for props. They entered communities unannounced and performed pieces only roughly developed. They had titles as simple as their means: “The Ogre Show," “The Bread Show," “The Shoe Show,” “The Bird Show.” This European caravan met with scant success. According to John Heilpern, who came along to write about the trip, more often than not a performance would end in frustration on the part of the actors and puzzlement from the villagers. Interestingly, at one point Brook asked Heilpern to help by writing simple scripts. Yet the basic commitment of the troupe to the spontaneous rendered his work moot: “The moment you move into improvisation, written sounds become irrelevant," he writes, having ex- perienced first-hand the incompatibility between written and oral composition.[3]

[3] Heilpern, 97, 156, 182.

Brook was willing to experiment with absence of structure to such an extent that he courted failure for himself and his actors. What is most impressive of all about this trip for me is not the outcome, but the attitude. Failure is inadmissable in a literary-technological society, but in oral culture--in the world of improvisation, of putting it together in the crucible of the moment-the tangible possibility of failure is what makes success possible. Failure is not the poison but the spice of oral composition.

We can conclude, however, from reading Heilpern's account of Brook's experiment, that even for improvisation, a certain level of structure is necessary. Eric Bentley, noting the dramatic excitement of Moreno's spontaneous psychodramas, readily picked out psychodrama's debt to form, and in fact, the techniques which facilitate this unpremeditated acting-out of one's life are extensive and complex. Our brief study of the Homeric-style bards confirms this observation. It was only by leaning on a highly structured mnemonic of linguistic formulas and story themes that the storytellers could (improvisationally) create their tales. In the case of Brook's actors, not only did they lack a coherent form for their improvisations, but the intercultural context meant that their audiences did not even have a framework for appreciating their failure (to some extent Heilpern's book, read by Western minds who could appreciate what the actors were trying to do, brought them belated recognition).

Compared to Brook's Africa performances, theNST groups discussed in chapters 4 and 5 practice improvisation within structured contexts. The experimental theatre groups set their improvisationally developed pieces for performance. Teachers of creative drama develop a method for their sessions. Therapeutic theatre groups follow a set pattern in their encounters with their populations. The comic-satiricactors perform structured theatre “games,” which, if not “rehearsed," are intensively practiced.

In Playback Theatre, as well, there is a corresponding adherence to a highly structured framework which consists of an opening, fluid sculptures, introductions, scenes, pairs, the closing, and so forth. And of course, the enactment of each dramatic segment also proceeds along structured lines. The scenes, for example, involve the interview, the setting-up, the enactment, and the acknowledgment.[4]

[4] The description of these elements is contained in the transcript in the appendix. The “acknowledgment”refers to a moment after the completion of a scene and before the actors return to their boxes when they look at the teller, “giving back” the story to him or her.

Victor Turner, who has formulated a widely respected concept of societal development termed “social drama," has some valuable observations on this question of structure. His model has four stages. First, there is a *breach* of the social order, whether over political leadership, from an external environmental circumstance, or whatever. This leads to a shared sense of *crisis*. The community responds with some sort of *redressive mechanism*, which can take a wide variety of forms. The final stage involves either the achievement of a new *integration* or the acceptance of permanent *rupture* and change. Now Turner's name for this process, social drama, leads one to conceive of it as a dramaturgical model for social interaction, like Goffman's. But for our purposes, it is the third stage, the redressive mechanism, which is of the greatest interest, for certainly in preliterate societies, it often takes the shape of drama. In other words, the social drama is resolved through dramatic ritual. This ritual is a complex event, providing an ecstatic experience which brings relief from community tensions, even while it brings to the forefront elements of tribal history useful in resolving the crisis. Furthermore, it is set up so that the enactment will leap beyond the procedure. “Rules ‘frame’ the ritual process,” writes Turner, “but the ritual process transcends its frame.” Thus ritual becomes a “trans- formative self-immolation of order."

In Bateson's language, the tribe sets up a framework for enactment so that it can supercede it. At that instant, a metacommunicative frame surrounding both original and new will encapsulate both and give the occasion meaning. The exciting part is that this “superframe” of understanding is never known beforehand. There is an atmosphere of uncertainty and unpredictability which Turner describes with the word "liminal."[5]

[5] See Turner 1981,40, 45, 85. Borrowing Paul Goodman's term,
Turner places special emphasis on “communitas" and liminality:
"The bonds of communitas are anti-structural in that they are undifferentiated, equalitarian, direct, nonrational (though not irrational), I-Thou or Essential We relationships (in Buber's sense)." He also says: “I see liminality as a phase in social life in which this confrontation between ‘activity which has no structure' and its ‘structured results' produces in men their highest pitch of self- consciousness." (Turner I974, 47, 255) Such comments might be describing nonscripted theatre. In applying Turner's model to Playback Theatre experience, I can think of “crisis" rituals, such as a performance for an individual who lost a leg in an accident, with his friends and family present; or a workshop which climaxed in the stories of a Vietnam veteran, a Weatherman, and others whose lives had been traumatized by war (this occasion is described in chapter 17). However, for the most part, it seeims that Playback works to heal a chronic alienation, the breach of modernism, if you will. In the face of too few opportunities for integrative rituals, Playback can provide “definitional” ceremonies (Myerhoff's term) which help build communitas. The term “redressive” in the preliterary drama paradigm is borrowed from Turner.

THE LIMINAL STATE

Spontaneity, then, to connect Turner's thinking with our discussion so far, can be thought of as a creative response to a liminal condition. Thus native Americans reaching adulthood went off into the wilderness in order to find their vision; they knew it could only come by means of deliberately incurring, through hunger and solitude, a liminal condition of perceptivity. What the Indian sought through “sacred songs and hardship" we moderns, attached to physical comfort, shirk. What answers they shared, we try to sell. We may prize the idea of transformation, but our rituals, says Turner, are “limi- noid” rather than liminal. One thinks of a Broadway play, or the typical American television sitcom, in which there is more a pretense of suspense, pathos, and humor than the genuine thing. That is what the public seems to want, or what the advertisers will give them.

What is most important, then, in discussing structure and freedom in the theatre is not that there must be structure, nor that there must be freedom, but that there be established a condition of liminality between the two states. Thus, seeing a play, with all its structure, can be a liminal experience, while seeing a happening, which is“spontaneous,” can be deadening. Any combination is possible so long as it falls within a spectrum at the extremes of which are the anomie of excessive freedom and the dispirited productivity of rigid structure. In discussing the Playback performance in chapter 4, we noted the oscillation between dramatic and everyday reality. Here the names at each end of the spectrum are different, but the process is the same. In fact, we shall return to it again and again.

Some of the most exciting moments in Playback Theatre performances have been when the totally unexpected occurs. For example, we have occasionally had tellers who try to manipulate the performers. One man got up and said he wanted to see if the actors could do what his theatre company could do back in Indianapolis. As conductor, I explained that we did not do quite that kind of thing, but perhaps he wanted to tell us a story about his group. No, he said, he wanted to see how good we were. No, I said. We're here to do your story. When he didn't shift, neither did I. In the end, I sent him back to his seat, and we did no story. The risk here is that abandoning our structure may lead to a breakdown of the performance; the audience may complain--or worse, walk out. Of course, I also know that to turn it over to the actors in such a situation is also risky, for there is no story to work from. In Turner's terms, the teller's breach produced a crisis, which necessitated some kind of action. In this case, I decided to let the structure break down. The man and I had a public encounter, instead. Insofar as my action was felt to be redressive by those present, the show did not suffer. In this case, it was enhanced. The atmosphere had been electric, while the audience saw the performers refuse to be patsies. Thus we can be grateful for the manipulator tellers, for their provocations provide us with a special kind of spontaneity test.

Let me give another example: once a woman told a story of an epileptic seizure during which she had fallen and injured her face. During the enactment, she leapt out of her seat sobbing uncontrollably and embraced the actor playing her. “I'm sorry, I'm sorry," she kept repeating. This sparked a high level of anxiety; some audience members even began to leave. But we did not abandon the scene. We brought the teller back to the chair, completed the scene and reworked it a number of times. In the end, she was laughing amidst her tears, and the audience also grew calm.

In both of these unexpected moments, one in which a teller refused to share a story, and the other in which a teller lost the sense of what she told as a “story,” we sought the right juncture on the Mood/Program spectrum. In the first, I let the “we'll do anyone's story” Program go completely, relying on my Mood sense that “this guy is tricking me." In the second, we did not give in to Mood concerns at all, but stuck to the Program and did her story.

When I visit other Playback Theatre companies, actors often express their frustration at not going as deeply into some stories as they sense is possible. I usually agree: I watch them render a teller's story, full of potential meaning, in such a way that it ends up being merely funny, or much too lengthy, or simply not clear. Often the root cause of this trivializing tendency is a fear of liminality. We fear risky situations if we lack sufficient confidence to deal with them--and rightly. Thus a new group, aware of its apprenticeship state with regards to the Playback method, will not take on high-pressure performances. And no matter how experienced they may be, actors who lack the personal philosophy and courage to consciously seek out a liminal moment will avoid confrontation with the heart of darkness in a story.

It is the Playback performer walking steadfastly towards liminality, towards either structured or free, sacred or profane, happy or sad, who can go all the way with a story. In the presence of such full spontaneity, audience attention is intense. 

To sum up, spontaneity first requires that the senses be open to information from the environment. To accomplish this receptor task, we must be *in* the moment, animal-like. Second, we must be able to stand *outside* the moment to make sense of what is occurring. We can then take action--that is, perform a conscious act--which is no small achievement. This action will in turn create a new environmental condition. Thus, spontaneity is the ability to maintain a free-flowing constantly self-adjusting cycle of sensory input, evaluation, and action. Bateson said “It is the attempt to *separate* intellect from emotion that is monstrous, and I suggest that it is equally monstrous--and dangerous--to attempt to separate the external mind from the internal. Or to separate mind from body."[6]

[6] Bateson, 464.

Spontaneity, then, means more than *quickness* of action. It means *choice* of action. This concept, existential in flavor, describes humankind dealing with a phenomenologically dynamic social universe which our need for meaning motivates us to try to understand. Faced with this condition, the human mind, or what Bateson refers to as Mind, has a remarkable ability for adaptation and invention. It is connected to our capacity for play, but calls upon our highest intelligence. Improvisational performance highlights this form of genius, reminding us publicly that spontaneity, in the fullest sense, is the inheritance of us all.
