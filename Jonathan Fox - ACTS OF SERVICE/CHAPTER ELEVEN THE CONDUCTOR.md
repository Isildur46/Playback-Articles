# CHAPTER ELEVEN THE CONDUCTOR

The conductor, half-director, half-actor, is a feature of every Playback improvisational performance. The conductor has no place in literary theatre, with its fourth wall and uninterrupted dramatic fiction, and conse- quently the role is not widely understood or even acknowledged. Sometimes he or she is called the master of ceremonies, or emcee; sometimes the coordinator. Joseph Chaikin utilizes the term “intermediary,” signifying liaison between actors and audience. Augusto Boal places his emphasis somewhat differently: he describes the function of a person called the “(oker,’ a figure who stands apart from the fictive reality. That the conductor is of central importance in Playback is immediately clear from the performance transcript in the appendix: the conductor has more lines than anyone else, is in the spotlight more than anyone else, communicates directly to the audience, invokes the Tellers, is a link between the teller and the actors and has ultimate authority for beginnings and endings. The conductor is more than anything else a conduit- for words, feelings, energies. He or she performs some of the same tasks associated with the orchestra conductor.

What about the conductor's place in other forms of non- scripted theatre? Let us briefly run down the list:

*The experimental theatre*, choosing for the most part to preserve fictive inviolability, generally avoids using a conductor. However, instances of improvisation utilizing audience input, such as the Open Theatre's post-naturalistic improvisations, clearly need an intermediary to field input from the audience. More frequent are quasi-conductor embodiments, such as Brook in Africa, sitting apart from the performing space--i.e., positioned so that he has perspective both on actors and audi- ence--making suggestions to the performers as they perform.

One of the conclusions Brook and his troupe came to was that they did not have the skills to handle the packed circle of excitable villagers which often surrounded them. On the other hand, they noticed that local performers could deal with similar situations easily. My guess is that the native performers, schooled in the oral tradition, were expert at moving back and forth between the everyday and fictive levels, while Brook's troupe, for all their experimentation, steadfastly clung to that fourth wall, even in the village square, even in the mid-day sun.[1]

[1] See Heilpem, 164., 218, 279. In fairness, the locals probably would not have been as experienced in the dramatic realm, capable of playing, as Brook's actors could, many different roles in many different styles. In fact, for many of them, the very idea of fiction might have been strange.

*Educational theatre* performances vary, but I would say that more often than not, an adult teacher-director will speak to the audience as him or herself (not in role), providing an introductory word or more substantive facilitation.

*The community theatre* usually makes use of a coordinator in performances, who helps make a smooth shift from fictive to everyday levels.

*The therapeutic theatre* does the same. Here the conductor role, however, is liable to include full responsibility for redressive outcomes. As such, the conductor in therapeutic theatre, often thought of as a therapist, is seen as the most important figure in the drama.

*In comic-satiric theatre*, the audience is asked for sug- gestions. An actor or actors will do the asking; at such moments, the performer turns conductor.

*In the circus world*, there are traditionally two conductors, both with a highly institutionalized function: the ringmaster, dressed in high hat and tails, whose job is to introduce the acts and keep the program moving; and the clowns, dressed in rags and motley, whose job is to frame the acts by virtue of the opposite sort of emphasis--silliness, chaos, and ineptitude.

An everyday illustration of the conductor as conduit or link occurs when a person introduces two people to each other. The moment always has a charge to it. This charge increases as the meeting appears more important--say with enemies being brought together, or introducing a guest speaker before a large audience. Almost everyone has this experience in one way or another with some frequency, and many do not particularly like it. It is a vulnerable position, for the encounter might end in disappointment-or even more unsettling, it might end in love. The introducer-conductor is also a role, despite its apparent unassuming nature, of tremendous personal power.

Alistair Cook, the host or “introducer” of the television programs Omnibus and Masterpiece Theatre, likened his job to that of a head waiter, a minor figure, he said, whose name was nevertheless widely known. In one sense he was right. After all, it was the plays and other presentations we turned on the television to watch. But in another sense, Cook was very important. He was our line towards and away from the experience; he was particularly knowledgeable about it, yet not totally *in* it and therefore trustworthy. Cook's head waiter self-characterization was most certainly over-modest; his learned, witty greetings and farewells provided a most valuable framing indeed.

In the course of this chapter I will draw on my own ex- perience as conductor in Playback Theatre to explain and ex- emplify the conductor's role. First, I would like to present a few non-Playback examples; in each case I was present, but not as the leader. The three examples below involve a person (I have changed the names) making an introduction, a teacher presenting a school music concert, and a singer-songwriter in performance.

*The Introducer*. I was far from home, about to start a workshop at a drama school. The directors of the school were away. It was left to the administrative secretary, Ursula, to introduce me to the twenty-five or so participants. It so happened that Ursula grew up in Papua-New Guinea and was familiar with oral declamatory style. Whether influenced by this knowledge or not, she began with a strong “Welcome!”to the participants, extending her hands as she did so. The words that followed, both to them and to me, combined formality and warmth. When I took over two minutes later, the room was already glowing.

*The Music Teacher*. When my daughter was in fourth grade, she attended an elementary school which had been attached to the local teachers'college. A decision had been made to close the school, a subject of sadness for many teachers, parents and children. Mr. Samuels, the music teacher, orchestrated a Christmas concert during the final year. Its scope was amazingly comprehensive--children of different grades sang and played instruments, in different combinations and styles. Mr. Samuels was a jazz musician. He did not hesitate to take the focus at the start and at various times during the program. His manner was informal and enthusiastic. He even joined in towards the end, playing with the children. Because of his involvement, the parents were able to enjoy the spirit of *playing* as much as the actual pieces performed. It was obvious the children were having a good time. As the concert came to a close, this teacher who was about to lose his job spoke again to the parents and children who were about to lose their school. He suggested we all stand and hold hands and sing the final number. It was very moving. Mr. Samuels reminded us of the idea of community which this school had come to embody by allowing us to experience it in the framework of the childrens' concert. Without a hint of direct allusion, he engaged us in a profoundly satisfying conspiracy of meaning.

*Singer-songwriter*. Bob White began his concert in the in- timate small-town hall with a small speech, which turned out to be a hilarious commentary on an item in that day's news. White had unusual presence. His pauses allowed us to really feel his energy, and for him to feel ours. And all evening, he made use of this type of communication, often speaking between numbers. Sometimes, like many singers, he spoke about the genesis of a song, but whatever his subject, we felt him to be very present with us. He was able to allude con- stantly to the here-and-now context, building, as the concert progressed, a kind of private story, or culture, unique to us. He did this even in his songs, which were obviously not improvised, but almost every chorus involved subtle variations which particularly connected with us in the audience. Finally, his art was such that his interaction did not take away from the power of his music: he was able to be a channel for us through talk as well as music. In fact, he was able to structure an oscilla- tion between the two which increased the intensity of our enjoyment.

None of these examples actually involve theatre, but then the conductor's role is applicable not only to theatre, but to a variety of performance and social contexts. For contrast, let us take one more instance, this time from a theatre performance:

*Community youth theatre facilitator*. An improvisational theatre group, comprised of teenage students taught by a husband and wife team, were performing a show called *Pre- dicaments*. The “play” consisted of a series of prepared scenes which presented teenage problems. After each five-to-ten minute skit, the actors froze. The facilitator, who was the wife, Maryann, then asked the mostly student audience for their thoughts on how the scene should end. In each case, she took the first suggestion, usually then allowing other spectators to embellish the plot somewhat. She echoed and clarified the suggested ending. Then the actors completed the story- improvisationally. There were five skits, intermission, five more, and then, with a thank you from Maryann, the show was over.

Now Maryann did a perfectly satisfactory job, in that she explained things clearly at the start, effected a smooth transition between prepared and improvisational segments, and closed off the proceedings at the end. Moreover, she was personable and able to relate to the young audience. But there was potential unfulfilled in her performance. First, she did not *perform* in the sense of using her body and voice to enliven the audience, as Ursula did with her declamatory welcome and out- stretched arms, or Mr. Samuels did, when he actually played music with the kids. Second, she played it safe emotionally, in that she left it to the skits to move the audience. She abdicated from whatever power *she* might also have had to reach the young people on an emotional level. Thus she did not take the most *true-to-life* response, or the most *heartfelt* response, but the “first” response, thereby absolving herself of any need to make on-the-spot judgments. Finally, nowhere did she inject her own wisdom or her own spontaneous energy. She remained steadfastly the “headwaiter,” and kept the shaman outside the door.

In Maryann's defense, the form put limitations on her scope as conductor. If audience participation is part of the format, as it clearly was in the performance of Predicaments, then it is important to be fair. “First answer, first served” is one way to be fair. But it limits the possibilities for strong intersection of fictive and social levels, for truth, and for creative interventions.
A better form would allow both for fairness *and* for incisive responses from the conductor.[2]

[2] *Predicaments* presented other limitations from an NST viewpoint. For instance, since each skit presented an unequivocal “problem,” there was little scope for emotional variability, even though the actors, in their improvisations, managed to interject wit into their serious solutions. The result was an increasing pressure in the course of presenting ten“problems” for (inadmissable) humor and a last skit that lost all touch with seriousness. Everyone was delighted, but it was a case of needing to abandon the Program, here a text organized in conceptual terms (undoubtedly an adult writer's idea of adolescent difficulties), in order to adapt to the never predictable Mood of the audience.

To sum up so far, the good conductor, an intermediary between actors and audience, has strong presence, connects the audience with feelings as well as thoughts, makes bold interventions and takes risks in the name of truth.

## CONDUCTOR AS DIRECTOR

There are three basic ways that the conductor is like a director:

*The conductor is an emcee*. In contrast to the literary thea- tre, what takes place in an NST performance is rarely totally preordained. Thus the conductor is agenda and time keeper, making certain that the basic ingredients of the performance are included, and that the show begins and ends well. In one sense this is a fairly easy job. After all, to take Playback as an example, all the conductor has to do is keep in mind a small list from among the possibilities of fluid sculptures, sound sculptures, scenes, pairs, and so forth. But in the speed of the improvisational moment, it can be hard to maintain the necessary detachment and remember, say, that we planned to have a music interlude after the third or fourth story, or that today we will do two scenes with puppets. Secondly, there are sometimes announcements which need to be made on the social reality level (“Please put your name on our mailing list;"“The
Museum has a special exhibit on at the moment;" "Judy will be running a workshop next Saturday for teenagers..."). The good conductor will be able to drop out of the *performance* sufficiently to remember these kinds of tasks. Thirdly, like an emcee, the conductor has particular responsibility for beginnings and endings. These can be particularly challenging. In fact, they almost *always* are. It will fall to the conductor to give the signal to begin, often in the face of practical crises and chaotic feelings which threatenthe successful transition into the show. And it falls to the conductor to end. Ending will often be opposed vigorously by the audience, and even at times by actors. On less successful occasions, the need of the performers might be to continue, in the hope of rescuing hidden fruit from dry stalks.

I remember one performance which had gone well. The last story had been told and acted out, and as conductor I was beginning my farewell. Quite a few people started to clamor for more. I had set the “ending frame”earlier by announcing that the previous story would be the last, but this time, I gave in, and invited one more teller to come. The young man who approached the chair told of an experience which did not fit in with what had gone before, and we all felt it. Somehow, it was a story outside the frame of the performance. This memory has stayed with me as an instance when I abandoned my emcee role.  Why did I make the wrong decision at that moment? Perhaps I craved approval, and under pressure did whatever the audience demanded. Actually, I think I had felt that it hadn't been such a great show and was also vulnerable to thinking, "just one more and we'll show you!”

But in the NST, beginnings and endings are always variable; they provide an opportunity for the conductor to execute the plan not only with efficiency, but with redressive potency. 

*The conductor is an expert in the form*. The conductor is captain of the team on stage, with special responsibility to know and use with integrity the particular dramatic approach being performed. Thus the conductor who is teacher of creative dramatics, the psychodrama director, and the emcee for comedy improv must each have a solid grasp of their method. The consequences of insufficient familiarity can be serious. Either the conductor will respond to the scare of a focal moment by freezing and making an inappropriate response, or perhaps worse, in response to the felt inexpertise of the leader, nothing much will happen at all (in the way of audience offers). This latter eventuality may not be so bad, for at least it is not dangerous, and it allows for neophyte conductors to learn their trade. Thus there is a built-in safety mechanism. If audience and actors do not feel safe in the hands of the conductor, they will not take risks. But of course, neither will there occur exciting drama.

Actors, of course, also must know the form.[3] The actors' involvement, however, is deepened by the presence of the conductor, whom they know will watch over them and provide guidance. The buck stops at the conductor.

[3] It takes a new Playback actor a full year to acquire basic knowledge of the form.

What does the conductor have to know? First of all, he must know the abc's of getting a story over to the actors. New conductors find they do not know how to elicit necessary information quickly; they do not know the formulas; they do not know how to help shape the scenes.[4]

[4] Too much stage directing from the conductor stifles the actors, whereas none can be inefficient as well as eliminating the conductor as an artistic participant in the shaping of the dramatization. For an example of conductor instructions to actor, see appendix (11. 552-3, 759-774).

The conductor must also know how to end a scene. He or she can choose whether to dismiss a teller or suggest further enactment of some sort. A wrong decision here will leave audience and actors feeling either unfinished or overcooked.

The conductor's function as custodian of the form is most central at moments of crisis, when the fate of an entire event will rest on a central, split-second decision. In the permeable atmosphere of NST, the form is liable to be threatened at any time, by a spectator-participant, by the environment, sometimes even by the actors.

Such a moment occurred when the epileptic teller leapt to the stage as previously described. In that moment she had lost her boundaries. The Playback form calls for tellers to remain in the chair, watching their story enacted. I brought her back to the chair, then continued with the enactment until she had taken sufficient possession of herself to appreciate the difference between *life*, in which she had injured herself, and play, in which she was now helping herself.

Audience members *will* challenge the conductor's role as custodian of the form. After all, NST audiences are encouraged to be spontaneous, to participate, to let go of literary theatre habits. Some people will respond to this invitation to liminality by provocation. It is only expectable. The conductor needs to be vigilant.

Audiences are not the only sources of such tests, however.
For example, it is in the spirit of NST to adapt to environmental conditions, but what about when it is *too* wild or *too* noisy? I recall an occasion when we  performed at a women's shelter Christmas party in a room full of young children. The activity and noisemaking was so intense we could barely do the stories. What gave an edge to the situation was that two distinguished visitors from overseas were present, watching Playback for the first time. For me as conductor, it was a real decision-point whether to try to do Playback at all(we could have filled in with songs and non-Playback skits, and I'm sure the crowd would have been happy enough). My pride was at stake, but we decided to go ahead, and we performed a much-interrupted, very ragged evening of Playback. Looking back, I would say we did the right thing, for in the midst of an environment which felt chaotic to me, there was much good telling and some good acting, too. As far as the guests were concerned, to my amazement, they seemed to appreciate what they had seen. One thing was clear that day-we were there for the audience; they were not there for us.

On the other hand, our first time in Australia, the management of the prestigious Nimrod Theatre gave us their main space for a late night show. Best of all the large hall was packed. I still remember making my entrance that night, exulting in the sight of all these people who had come to see Playback Theatre. But three hundred was too many for the intimate needs of the Playback form. That night we had an anecdotal story about a possum on the roof. This was followed by one about a kookaburra on the window sill. Of course any story has meaning, but this night meanings were hard to find. I think most of the stories in fact meant: “In front of all these laughing people I am not going to tell anything important... what can I think of... I do want to tell something and be seen... oh, yes, I'll tell the one about...” It is not surprising that the Australian arts council, which had representatives in the audience, dismissed Playback as superficial. As custodian of the form, I would have done better perhaps to refuse to play the Nimrod (at least at our then current level of skill at understanding and interpreting stories; today I look back on those stories, elusive in the haze of forgetfulness, and imagine that the themes of that evening, with their highlighting of ordinary humans interacting with the natural world, were in no way superficial in the Australian context).

Actors can threaten the form if they act badly. However, that may not be specifically the conductor's responsibility. In Playback this question arises each time there is consideration of including an audience-up segment in a performance. The audience actors are for the most part total newcomers to the form. But they often do remarkably well. Their scenes are almost always well received, for the remaining spectators respond to the Playback performers' willingness to share their art and the audience performers' courage to try it. However, audience-up scenes can be long and disjointed, an interruption in a show's momentum. Is allowing the audience to be actors allegiance to the form, or abandonment of it? Playback groups in different cities have different opinions on this question, which raises yet another: is not a “form” an organic body of knowledge and experience that is in constant development and change? Thus the conductor must be not only an expert on past practice, but capable of forging the new.

*The conductor is a cultural ambassador*. Although it will vary greatly depending on the type of nonscripted form, the conductor never takes on a dramatic role and consequently is never totally free of social exigencies, such as relating to the leader(s) of the host organization, if the audience is a specific group. Moreover, insofar as NST is not always clearly under- stood, contact with key community individuals may be important to help ensure a sympathetic reception for a performance or workshop.

Once Playback was hired for a performance as part of a program for secretaries. “Many of these women experience sexual harassment on their jobs,” said the organizer: “I want you to do something on that theme.” We were talking on the telephone. I answered that we would be glad to do stories on that theme if they came up, but our method was to leave it to the secretaries to choose their own themes. Such a statement, and the explanation why, obviously can be made by someone other than the conductor.)Yet as the future conductor I began a relationship with the organizer during that telephone call, for I knew that the extent to which we were allies or enemies would be significant when the time came for the show. Thus the phone call, my greeting her upon arrival on the day of the performance, our words together, our interaction during the show, and our encounter afterwards were all part of my conducting task. She had invited us to undertake a ceremony that had more power than she realized; moreover, I made it clear that this power was not hers to manipulate. The community would use it to find their own truths. My “ambassadorship” helped in her education about Playback Theatre and what it could accomplish.

A youth bureau director came to see a Playback team work with a teen group as part of a grant funded by her agency. The kids told stories about illicit smoking in the woods and
Saturday night parties. The director was critical of the “shallowness” of a method which allowed kids to tell about socializing when their lives contained grave problems. The situation here is a common one, but no less problematic for all that. In such cases, I often try to get the outside observer to tell a story, for then they begin to understand the holographic nature of a Playback Theatre story--how *any* scene can provide a sense of the teller's existential condition. I did not succeed in this case.

Let me give one more example of the conductor's community development potential. We did a series of tent per- formances in small towns in cooperation with the County De- partment of History. Before each performance, I went to the communities in question and met with members from the local historical society. The purpose of these meetings was to glean many bits of information and get a flavor of the life of the community that I could later use in the performance. Secondly, a significant segment of the local population had a chance to meet me. By the time of the performance, they knew more or less what we were about, and that I was trustworthy. So they came, brought others, and told their stories. And our “tribe” was able to interact with theirs.

## THE CONDUCTOR AS ACTOR

The conductor usually stands apart from the dramatic action, as coordinator, expert, personal liaison officer. However, he or she also stands *inside* the dramatic circle along with the actors, and as such, is an actor, too.[5]

[5] In an unpublished paper entitled “The Playback Conductor, or How Many Arrows Will I Need,” Mary Good, founder-director of the Playback company in Melbourne, Australia, separates the conductor's job into four main role headings: producer, social investigator, listener/communicator, and guide.

If we look at the performance transcript, we can observe that even whiorle the conductor is performing the emcee task of welcoming and explaining the procedure, he or she is performing an induction, in which the audience is being en- chanted by words whose repetitiveness, alliteration, and syntactical irregularity seduce the auditor from critical thought(See 11.5-15,97-114 of the performance transcript). The preliterary drama paradigm can help us appreciate that part of the conductor's task is to enchant. As such, any trance-inducing behavior may be appropriate, and each individual within each NST format will have a particular style. Dancing will be O.K., music will be O.K. Poetry, clowning around--perhaps espe- cially clowning around-will further the degree of transpor- tation. For a comparison, one can imagine the Mediterranean rhapsodes, transporting a crowd through realms of remembrance and longing, or the Kung dancers in kia, throwing themselves on the fire and inspiring others into ecstatic states. The conductor who is not also an actor is in this sense missing out on the potential of the role.

Most importantly, the function of the conductor is shamanistic, leading actors and audience in the direction of what Cole calls the “illud tempus,” that locus of meaning and rejuvenation which we often think of as a paradisiacal Eden but whose actual rediscovery is fraught with uncertainty. It is easy to pretend to find it; the theatre often does, producing a sentimental play of the kind Brecht and other critics have railed against. Furthermore, even though we may yearn for grace, we resist the unlayering that is the first step towards finding it.
Thus the conductor must utilize all his or her guile and power to catalyze an encounter with this source which is “not everyday," and “not pretend,” but arrived at only through a skillful blend of everyday and pretend. This is why Schechner's description of the shaman as being “at an extreme edge" is apt for the conductor. The conductor is a kind of perpetual pioneer, leading a band of hearty souls into the Wild, Wild West of experience.

In the Playback context, the conductor/shaman magically invokes a soul-shaking scene. The conductor stops the teller just at that moment when truth is touched and run away from. The conductor suddenly alters the order of the program in order to seize an inspiration.

Some examples: an Israeli woman tells a story about  her childhood in which her parents are unresponsive to her.
Something about what she is telling makes me ask if her par ents are Holocaust survivors. When she says, “Yes,” I suggest we include her parents'past in her story. The result was a profound piece of theatre for teller, actors, and audience.

During a performance, a woman tells a pair about her pregnancy--how she feels both afraid and terribly excited to give birth. I feel the depth of her response, as well as the connection between her and other women in the hall. Suddenly I am inspired to invite only men up onto the stage who want to take a woman's role at some stage in the pregnancy cycle. Many men came forward, enacting (with some help) a pelvic examination, cravings, the first kick, the physical discomfort of late in term, and so forth. The result was a wonderfully entertaining and poignant moment of role reversal, and the(mostly) women in the audience howled with cathartic glee.

Here is an example of reaching in the right direction, but ultimately failing to fulfill the shaman's task: at another performance, after I had announced the final story, a paraplegic man wheeled himself to the teller's chair and proceeded to tell a confusing story about his sex life. I got the story over to the actors as quickly as I could, the actors did the scene, and we ended the show. However, the note we ended on was definitely gloomy. There was a feeling among some that this teller had somehow sabotaged the show-even that it would have been good not to let him tell his story.

My analysis of the situation was distinctly different. Our philosophy encourages anyone to tell, and if an isolated individual, after sensing the climate of respect, takes courage to tell his story, then that is a tribute to the form, not a weakness of it. What was the essence of this teller's story of seduction? Was it, “I am handicapped, but I can be a teller, too?” It might have been: “I am handicapped, but I can have sex and be a man, too." Or it might have been the opposite: “I am handicapped. I feel lonely and excluded, in society and in romance. Please understand." I was not able to sense the meaning because of my own sense of uneasiness, especially in light of the subject of the story. Questions arose that I didn't dare ask, such as whether he was capable of having intercourse. My embarrassment, and those questions, were undoubtedly shared by the audience, many of whom may have been less ready than I to sympathize. So here was an opportunity for me to be bold in two kinds of ways. One was to ask whatever needed to be asked about the paraplegic condition (after all, he wanted to tell). The second was to deal with the audience's reaction, using my full author- ity, in the hopes that by the time we had enacted this teller's story, we would have felt enlarged. To achieve this end might have taken thirty minutes instead of twelve.[6]

[6] It is not uncommon that isolated individuals ask to be tellers right at the end. They wait and see and finally trust enough, as I have suggested. There is also undoubtedly an element of testing, if not pathology, at work, for by waiting until the very last minute, the chance of miscarriage increases. Thus in addition to presenting a challenge on the fictive level--for example, how to enact *sympathetically* the radical lesbian's story of a communal ritual involving the killing of a rabbit--such tellers often test us socially: Will we let them tell, will we take time with them, will we understand? Failure on this level repeats the syndrome of their interaction with society when our redressive mission demands that we transform it.

This instance also illustrates another important aspect of the conductor's experience: here the emcee, who was wrapping up the show, and the shaman, who sensed a major complication which would take time to deal with, were at odds. In the moment I gave in to the emcee, when I wished I had honored the shaman.

The conductor who boldly fulfills the shaman role will be seen by the audience as an actor, albeit a special kind of one. Like an actor, the conductor is “in” the performance, but because his or her continual responsibility is to achieve metalevel understanding of each moment of the performance experience, this trance is of a different nature than that of the actors'. In my experience, the aftermath is likely to be more tenacious, often lasting up to 24 hours afterwards. It is as if the effort required to master such paradoxical duties as boundary-setter and truth-seeker, enchanter and explainer, drama-bringer and social talker takes a special toll. As I have come to expect this aftermath, my warm-up to conducting is affected. For days before the event I am liable to feel the tension of needing a playful spirit for such hard work.[7]

[7] For a discussion of some aspects ofNST performance and shamanism, see Pendzik, 81-92. See also Larsen. For Larsen, shamans, in order to practice, must possess magic power, a guiding or healing mission, and a community (personal communication, January 26, 1978).

In summary, this discussion of the conductor has focussed on combined tasks of protocol, coordination, expertise, enchantment and shamanism. I contend that virtually all NST forms will benefit from a true conductor. One of my criticisms of educational theatre has been a tendency to deny the shaman aspect of the teacher's role. This denial has often taken shape in terms of an avoidance of the personal, or psychological. But is not the personal precisely where meaning often resides? If little Suzie's father has recently left home and is living in
Florida, and you happen to know it, and if Suzie does a spontaneous drama about Florida oranges, you have an oppor- tunity. The pusillanimous teacher will ignore the privileged information. After all, Suzie may get upset, which would be unfortunate, and then the class might get disturbed, which would definitely be uncalled for.

But the teacher who is also a conductor will dare to help Suzie make the connection between her feelings and her words, will risk the challenge of dealing openly with divorce and separation, will even feel that the whole class might benefit from such candor. A hand on the shoulder might be enough for Suzie to feel at ease doing her “Oranges" play. But if more is needed, then the teacher/shamanwill be unafraid to act, doing whatever is necessary to bring Suzie and her classmates to a redressive outcome.

Are such situations unusual? Not at all. They happen every day, every hour, in every context.

The combination of functions demanded by the conductor is not something anyone can do. We are hardly taught in school to develop the wit and foolishness of a jester (Boal's “joker"), who dares comment on experience while in the midst of it. Or to embody Brecht's verfremdung principle even while our senses are open to the very feelings and invisible emana- tions *verfremdung* was conceived to overcome. Nor, in fact, are we even schooled to be Matthew Arnold's “alien,” uncompromisingly devoted to promoting the idea of “best self" and for “seeing things as they are."[8] The conductor is potentially all of these, standing before the communal throng, demanding, commanding, instructing, exhorting us to embrace existence in its fullness, insisting on redressive outcome despite ubiquitous lassitude and fear.
