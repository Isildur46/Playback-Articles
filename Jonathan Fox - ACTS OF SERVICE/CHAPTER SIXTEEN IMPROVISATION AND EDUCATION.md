# CHAPTER SIXTEEN IMPROVISATION AND EDUCATION

Many actors, new to improvisation, show their resis- tance to the demands of spontaneity by constantly judging themselves and others. So we emphasize for them “not being critical.” Spectators exhibit the same tendency in their ignorance of the differences between NST and literary theatre: NST demands joining in, experiencing, a blurring of the lines between art and life--suspending, in fact, habits of critical judgment developed for plays. Furthermore, an NST theatre experience may stir feelings in a nonintellectual way that is particularly discomfiting in our rational-minded age; in this sense, everyone is liable to come to the work with a predisposi- tion to critical thinking that is a barrier to full appreciation.

That being said, it is evident from the discussion in the preceding chapter that I feel theatre is defined by thinking. Paradoxically, critical judgment, as well as the absence of it, is necessary for the practice of NST. Judgment forms the basis of standards; standards make possible excellence, the general subject of this chapter.

The question of standards is related to the idea of culture, a concept of particular significance for those charged with preserving and promoting it--critics, intellectuals, arts council administrators, and teachers. In this book we have used the concept of culture in four senses, and it will be useful to delineate them. The most frequent usage of “culture” has been in the anthropological sense, meaning *a set of values, practices, and traditions*, as in “primal,” “Viennese,” “Oriental,” “pretechnological,” or “thought” culture. Our understanding of “oral” and “literary” culture has been formed by a related connotation suggesting levels of social development; in this sense literary culture is later than, and a development from, oral culture.

A second sense is the Freudian concept of those restraining customs and laws that help us in the “non-gratification of powerful instinctual urgencies.”We touched on this area in our discussion of safety and freedom (chapter9), which stressed the destructive possibilities of unregulated action.

A third sense is that associated with *arts and education*, “high” culture, or what Matthew Arnold means when he writes that the way to culture is through “the best that has been thought and said in the world."[1] It is this kind of culture that Antonin Artaud sought to replace with a less refined, more demonic “hunger;” it is the Bastille of this culture, identified with the upper classes, that socialist community arts proponents, such as Owen Kelly in the United Kingdom, wish to storm.

[1] Arnold, 6.

Finally, we have promulgated a concept of culture defined by spontaneity, which values above all a *situation-centered creativity*--the innovative and continual rediscovery of the world, rather than the preservation of established and accepted forms. Every group will have its own spontaneous culture, uniquely defined by its constituents and unbeholden to outside convention. In the spontaneity view of culture, there is a con- comitant appreciation of the dynamic nature of social life, according to which leadership will shift continually in search of the idea which connects and includes.[2]

[2] For the theoretical basis for spontaneity, Moreno remains an important source. See Fox 1987.

Greater acceptance of nonscripted theatre as theatre might lead to fruitful developments towards improvement of standards. First of all, NST can benefit from criticism. For the most part, it is ignored by critics, who are staunchly literary- minded. Moreover, nonscripted theatre needs attention from critics with some knowledge and appreciation of the distinct objectives of improvisational theatre production. Secondly, arts funding sources need to expand their guidelines to include nonscripted practitioners; at present without a strong ethnic identity (in which case there may be a special category of funding), it is almost impossible (in the USA) for a process- oriented ensemble to receive funding as a theatre company. Thirdly, there is need for research: to develop new methods for the documentation and analysis of oral theatre; to study the levels of context-text related communication as they become manifest in an improvisational performance; to examine the spontaneous creative process of the improvisational actor (the results might have usefulness far outside the field of theatrical studies). Most needed are historical and comparative studies of nonscripted theatre. These should include studies of the transition between preliterary and literary as well as the interstices between literary and postliterary genres. Exactly what actual connections have there been between the various NST practitioners, both horizontally and vertically? I am fascinated by what appears to be a central European hot spot in the germination of the NST movement: were the progenitors Goethe, Laban, Brecht, Moreno, and colleagues as yet unknown?[3] Or in fact, has there been *throughout* Europe a long tradition of unscripted productions of various kinds, a tradition simply carried on by the above as well as teachers like Dorothy Heathcote, Jacques Lecoq, the French animateurs? Finally, students of drama need educating on the distinctions between nonscripted and literary enactment; on the aesthetic vs. redressive purposes of theatre; on theatre's roots in the small community vs. its place in the modern city; and on that narratizing, self-reflexive communication of meaning that defines theatre's boundaries.

[3] “A major current in Austrian national theatre [at the turn of the century] was the 'magic farce,' a kind of fairy-tale pantomime which was at the same time a pedagogic instrument," write Allan Janik and Stephen Toulmin in *Wittgenstein's Vienna*. (page 76) A recent New York *Times* article (September 3, 1987) reports on a *Stegreiftheater*, or “Ad Lib Theatre,” in Vienna, as the last of what numbered about 40 improvisational theatre groups in that city early in the century. These are fascinating tidbits. See also Schmidt-Ranson. 

## EDUCATION AND THE ACTOR

As for the NST practitioners themselves, the rough and spontaneous nature of NST improvisations might seem to make moot the question of the actor's education. As Theseus said in defending his decision to allow Bottom and company to do their skit in *Midsummer Night's Dream*, “Never can anything be amiss when simpleness and duty tender it." True, authenticity is key. The fact is, however, most of us are not Bottoms or Snugs. We have been born into an age of accessible art and benefited from thirteen to twenty-odd years of formal schooling. We have read books, seen films and plays, have immediate access to the world's political, social, and cultural developments and benefitted from informed commentary to help us understand and reflect on events.

Culture, whether in the anthropological or literary sense, places great value on tradition. Formal education teaches us. both skills of thinking and knowledge of the tradition. In the literary theatre, playwright and director assume most of the responsibility for this cultural knowledge. In improvisation, however, as we have discussed, actor and playwright are one. Therefore cultural knowledge must come from the actor him- or herself. In such a context, is it enough for the improvisational performer to depend only on heartfeltness and flow? And if not, what are reasonable expectations with regard to the learning of an NST performer?

Ideally, he or she will know the stories of the culture. This means the children's stories--the myths, fables, and fairy tales that help shape our understanding of the world.[4] It also may mean, depending on the actors, the audience, and the particular NST form, knowledge of adult stories--current events, classic and contemporary books, history.

[4] “Regarding this task [restoring meaning to the lives of disturbed children], nothing is more important than the impact of parents and others who take care of the child; second in importance is our cultural heritage, when transmitted to the child in the right manner." (Bettelheim, 4)
 
He or she will be an educated user of the language, with a large vocabulary, a sense of correct usage, and a familiarity with poetry, the phrases and rhythms of which wed high consciousness with our bicameral memories.

He or she will be familiar with the great questions of human experience investigated by philosophers and scholars. 

He or she will have a good general education.

Such knowledge will mostly remain invisible. But it will constantly inform the decisions and expression of the NST performer, permitting embarrassing lacunae to be avoided, and enabling what would otherwise be a merely adequate presenta- tion to become both magical and incisive. The conductor needs such education even more than the actors, because it is a unique role both directorially and as a channel.

When improvisors must do a scene which takes place at a performance of *Aida*, at a university lecture, during the Depression, or during a reading of “Dick Whittington," they are at a marked disadvantage if they are too unfamiliar with opera, intellectual discourse, historical conditions of the I930s, or the geographical and cultural importance of London.

## WELL-CHOSEN WORDS

Let me cite one more instance from the playtext. It occurs at the conclusion of Ben's story (II. 245-252). A father has come home to find his sons, Mark and Jason, on the verge of an explosive fight, but through talking and walking the father(who is the teller) calms down the older boy:

    BEN: Maybe the fighting won't happen as much now. Maybe we can all relax now. 
    MARK: You know, if *he* would talk to me like this, it would be different.
    BEN: I work a lot, you know. It's hard for me... to take the time. But I love you. Very much.
    MARK: I guess I even love Jason.
    BEN: Let's go home. (*They turn towards house. Ap- plause*.)

Here the line “I guess I even love Jason," as a tag to the scene, is unnecessarily explicit and has a saccharine taste. The improvising actor may be showing sensitivity as he tunes into his character's underlying affection for his younger sibling, but he betrays ignorance of the narrative mode, in which the same sentiment might better have been expressed indirectly. Extensive on-the-spot NST practice will teach skills in play writing, but it will benefit an actor to know something about literary expression (in the sense of the writing of stories) beforehand. Thus it would not be inappropriate for NST actors to write short stories, plays, even novels, as a part of their training.(In the chapter on actors, as well as in our discussion of spontaneity, I placed emphasis on personal presence and role flexibility. Added to these capacities is now the necessity for a good general and literary education. Without it, NST performances, and the very forms themselves, will be second-rate, no matter how infused with“simpleness and duty."

Unfortunately, manyNST actors are not particularly qualified in this regard, for perhaps two reasons. First, while at the extreme ends of the economic spectrum there may be individuals who express themselves well orally (either with erudition, wit, and grammatical purity, or with folksy, flowing, street-smart vitality), the majority of us receive an education consisting of what Ivan Illich calls a “stew of leftovers," leaving us both third-rate writers and thinkers and deracinated from oral styles as well. Secondly, the reactionary posture of many practitioners against literary values has produced a form of primitivism, unfortunate not due to its admiration of astrology, Native American culture, or love, but to the mindlessness with which such interests are pursued. A self-conscious cultural poverty and critical blindness is just as deadening as a self- conscious cultural exhibitionism. I find this development also in the humanistic psychology movement, which is in many ways allied with the NST. The issue is not only one of taking a stance towards “culture.” There is a philosophical issue, as well, for rejecting Arnoldian culture too often implies a simplistic world view that ignores vast terrains of human knowledge and reflection. When NST performers put blinders on, whether in the name of optimism, awareness of problems, or a dogmatic politics, they are limiting the impact of their work on thoughtful human beings.[5]

[5] David Cole, for example, is critical of participatory theatre and concept productions, which he feels are “an attempt to revitalize the theatrical event by means which in fact abolish it." I would argue that NST presentations based on inferior intellectual input deserve such criticism, but that NST by definition does not mean impoverished language and a destroying of “the theatrical event.” (Cole, 175)

All this is a tall order. If we come to the NST inadequately prepared, we face years of study and development. This is all right, and quite appropriate, sinceNST groups aim to last for years. However, it does run counter to emphases elsewhere on pragmatic, short-term training. The payoff for such dedication will be this: conductors and actors will rise to shamanic stature, able to connect mundane individual experience with archetypal themes and bring to present consciousness the wisdom and experience of those who have come before.[6]

[6] Education is often associated with pride, but to immerse oneself in the wisdom of others is an exercise in humility that the unread often do not understand. The spontaneity process works to integrate past and present thought as well as other frames of reference.

Finally, I would like to comment briefly on the development of an NST company's dramatic format.

In NST, a group will usually have “invented" their par- ticular dramatic presentation, although there will almost always be a web of connections with the NST “tradition.”Furthermore, in a context where the form may be even more distinct than the improvised text, this form will be in a state of evolution. With Playback, for example, we began with the idea of enacting people's stories, and did simply that. In time we developed a variety of ways to do it, including fluid sculptures, sound sculptures, pairs and scenes. We also added music. In the process, we incorporated lighting, stage dress, props. Our equipment steadily, if slowly, increased.

Was this development a betrayal of the cause of poor theatre, of the natural simplicity embodied in the idea of environmental attunement? My answer is No, we simply made judicious use of modern technology to serve our purpose. Certainly, there is no one answer; each particular group, and each concept, needs to find its own way. And while NST theatricality will tend to come from the performers or from use of natural settings rather than from artifice, again, I do not feel it is smart to ignore on principle centuries of theatrical invention. An even bigger mistake than summary rejection of the technology of the theatre is, in my view, to proceed in ignorance of it. Critics are right to expect from NST groups a basic knowledge of theatrical tradition. The NST practitioner enters the field as a citizen of the twentieth century, whose ideas will be enriched from the contribution of, say, Gordon Craig to lighting design, or the French modernists to stage decor, or the more recent innovations in environmental theatre space. Nonscripted theatre is a product of modern as much as of ancient culture, demanding that its creators be as knowledgeable as they can in the conscious evolution of their form.
