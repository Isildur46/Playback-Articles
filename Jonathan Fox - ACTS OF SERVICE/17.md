# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN THEATRE AND SOCIAL ECOLOGY

Memories of my fifteen-year life in the theatre fan out before me: times of triumph when audiences rose to their feet; times when I was so scared about an upcoming engagement, I popped awake at 4 a.m. for days before; times of total involvement in the moment; of deep laughter; of grief; of achieving a state so intensely different from the everyday, it took me hours and hours to “recover." At times I was like Coleridge or Keats, wondering, when it was all over, what life could be in the wake of such rapture. At other times I felt like Black Elk after a vision dance, looking exultingly on an altered world.

This has been another great joy: to be part of a family of performers (intentional, not inherited), sharing our work, our jokes, our physicality, our private joys and sorrows, our public testings. What has been especially meaningful has been to be with Jo, Judy, Carolyn, Michael, Bruce, Susan, Gloria, Danielle, Pete, Dan, Scotty, and Vince week in and week out, from season to season, year after year. In fact, I have been with most of these people longer than my parents were married to each other. For me, it was indeed like another family, satisfying in its particular mix of social and instrumental, private and public, constructive and idiotic. A further blessing has been the richness of the stories we have told and acted out: my own, my group's, our public's. I remember a story Sherrie told, even before the establishment of the Playback Theatre company, when the idea was in its most rudimentary testing stage. It was about her embarrassment in getting lost on the way to her therapist, and having to call him from a gas station. A funny story, yes, but also more. It was like all those stories when clear skies suddenly turn dark, where an easy ride becomes an ordeal, where dark holes appear from nowhere to engulf us. I have thought of this story many times over the years, when I am lost, or on the verge of lost. Sherrie's story has become one of my fairy tales. In recollecting it, I also remember that we can think of the events of our life as a story. This sort of narratizing has been important in my life. It has made me a more capable person than if I were like Paolo Freire's back country villagers, without critical consciousness, drifting inescapably from experience to experience. To be honest, I did this before Playback. In fact, my attachment to story played a big part in leading me to Playback. However, the sheer number of Sherries, and Toms, Geraldos, and Shikilas whose stories I have been part of since my involvement with Playback has given me a huge treasure hoard of wise and funny stories to live by, as well as connection with the tellers.

Why has the turnover been slow in the Playback company? I imagine it is for these reasons: to be in Playback brings emotional excitement, community, and stories to live by. I could search long for a better god.

Nevertheless, being part of this theatre has not been without its difficulties. The main one, despite the vivid presence of cohesion and meaning, is an enduring sense of alienation.

It has been difficult because Playback does not fit into a category. The theatre critic does not review it. The arts council is respectful, but can find no niche for it within its guidelines. The school administrator and the social service agency look for more specialized experience and education than we can show. Even the public is confused, coming up after a show and asking with genuine puzzlement, “What *is* this, anyway? What is your goal?”

It has been difficult in some circles because as a theatre company we work as a team. The organizers of the growth center, or the professional conference, for example, want a “theatre” experience, but possess a “lecturer" consciousness, finding it awkward to pay for more than one teacher, or even list more than one person in the catalogue of presenters.

It has been difficult becauseeven though we work outside the proscenium theatre, our work is dramatic. In performance, we need space to move. We make noise. We invite audience groups to move, make noise. Social institutions are housed in buildings that have relatively small rooms, filled with furniture. One is not supposed to disturb the neighbors (even in therapeutic establishments, where lip service is paid to the idea of catharsis). In most buildings, there is no space for the community ritual (save for an oversized auditorium, often unsuitable for NST demands for intimacy and interaction). For the first few years, we rehearsed in a church hall. We were thoroughly responsible, but over time our welcome there diminished, because in the end, the Episcopalians did not really like our free ways. So we moved to an arts facility--a museum--where it was easier, but not totally, for the effect of our work was to interfere with any other activity (always quiet, usually talking) that might be taking place in the building.

It has been difficult because our work is so personal and inherently emotional. As we go about the community, there is a fear, sometimes hidden, often explicit, about the openness of the Playback process to catharsis. The “What is this *for*?" question is often an expression of this type of concern. We are often accused of doing therapy. The therapists themselves express anxiety that an individual, in telling the story, may get too “opened up.” Related to this fear of feeling is a need for analytical frameworks. We are hired not for an experience, but for a “training." Written material-proposals, handouts, evaluations--is requested. “Outcomes” need to be predicted. Because it is process-oriented, there is a sense of something “soft”about the Playback concept. We are storytellers in a thought society, jesters in an all-work world. We are nurturing, caring, concerned to create beneficent atmospheres, while our employers place value on more traditionally masculine rubrics.

## THE SEARCH FOR COMMUNITY

Lastly, it has been difficult because for a group devoted to the community, the community has constantly proved elusive. The original Playback Theatre was founded in Dutchess County, New York, a suburban-rural area 75 miles from New York City. Its largest city, Poughkeepsie, has about 40,000 inhabitants. We performed there and in smaller towns throughout the region. Our audiences rarely exceeded 50. Often they barely reached 25, and over the years, despite a band of supporters, they did not get larger. In our efforts to find audiences, we turned our focus away from box office occasions and geographical communities towards instrumental associations-already organized groups, such as classrooms of children, halls full of teachers, participants at a workshop, prison inmates. What was frustrating here was that we came and went as “strangers," as it were. It did not feel like our community.

Why did we not locate in New York City, with its millions of potential supporters? After all, theatre has always been associated with urban congregations. The city affords the specialization and leisure to make art possible. That was just the point. We sought a theatre not limited by the concept of art, or even plays. It is the literary theatre that has grown up with the city. I turned away from the city-and its voracious credo of competition, self-consciousness, and aggrandizement. Even so, we have performed in New York City many times. What stands out about our box office performances there is the extent to which audience members do not feel in community with each other and tell to be seen, to make an impression, to give us a test. Their ego is in the way of our purpose. NST may build community, but a certain level of community must be present for the process to work.[1]

[1] Fernand Braudel, in his epic study of civilization, emphasizes the association of urban culture and writing, the “self-conscious" aspect of urban identity, and the bleeding of the village that bolstered the growth of towns and cities. My personal experience as an NST performer confirms the findings of sociologists (such as Redfield) of a dichotomy between folk and urban culture. NST values seem allied to the village. It is true, however, that some of the most successful Playback Theatre companies have established themselves in large cities, such as Sydney and Melbourne, where they seem to benefit from a large population base and the existence of a face-to-face culture.

The fact is, the preliterary village is gone forever from our society, and whatever dream we may have of being actors for our people must take a different shape than the images conjured up in anthropology books. We have tried one idea after another, opting for a smaller and smaller scoped definition of community.

The problem is made no easier by the changing and apparently elusive nature of “community” in mobile, media- dominated American society.

The modern “culture of separation" has been widely analyzed; it reaches from intellectual through popular ranks, from city to all but the smallest villages. Various treatments have been proposed, among them a new appreciation of what Robert N. Bellah and colleagues call “communities of memory:"

    Communities..have a history--in an important sense they are constituted by their past--and for this reason we can speak of a real community as a “community of memory,” one that does not forget its past. In order not to forget that past, a community is involved in retelling its story, its constitutive narrative, and in so doing, it offers examples of the men and women who have embodied and exemplified the meaning for the community. These stories of collective history and exemplary individuals are an important part of the tradition that is so central to a community of memory. 
    
    The stories that make up a tradition contain conceptions of character, of what a good person is like, and of the virtues that define such character. But the stories are not all exemplary, not all about successes and achievements. A genuine community of memory will also tell painful stories of shared suffering that sometimes create deeper identities than success... And if the community is completely honest, it will remember stories not only of suffering received but suffering inflicted-dangerous memories, for they call the community to alter ancient evils. The communities of memory that tie us to the past also turn us toward the future as communities of hope. They carry a context of meaning that can allow us to connect our aspirations for ourselves and those closest to us with the aspirations of a larger whole and see our own efforts as being, in part, contributions to a common good.[2]

[2] Bellah et al., 153. The term “social ecology”used in the chapter title is taken from them.

The authors of the above contend that Americans are in danger of forgetting certain essential values which shaped American identity in the formative years of the nation's history. Is it possible for a people, bereft of communities of memory, to forsake their story, and in the process lose themselves?  The im- portance of remembrance was also cited by Barbara Myerhoff in her work with the elderly. She charted the crises and ceremonies of senior center life, the “definitional”experiences. “Reminiscence is no mere escapist desire to live in the past, as some claim; rather it should be regarded as a major develop- mental task for the elderly, resulting in the integration that will allow them to age well and die well.” Further, she says, and not only about the old: “Audiences, listeners, witnesses are essential for self-awareness."[3] Finally, let us recall Richard Katz, the psychologist who studied the Kalahari healing dances. He concluded that in the maintenance of mental health in Kung society what mattered most were “qualities of the heart," compared to the modern Western mental health officer, whose “intellectual skills are stressed.” His conclusion for the West: “The community psychiatrist is sorely in need of a community healing ritual."[4] For me it is interesting that both Bellah and Katz focus on the need for healing community events.

[3] Myerhoff,222.

[4] Katz, 305.

Theatre can provide this function for society. It always has, in preliterary contexts and in our own mixed times of plays and NST. It is heartening to see that the rigidities of category may be melting somewhat. When Broadway shows involve audience input and utilize actors whose authenticity matters as much as their professionalism; when in their conceptualizations experi- mental theatre directors include not only other (oral-oriented) cultures, but also other (oral-oriented) theatre forms from their own culture; when social institutions--hospitals, businesses, institutions of higher learning--start to hire theatre groups to help celebrate definitional moments, one can imagine that we may be arriving at a new acceptance of that *balanced perceptivity* for which theatre can be seen to be a primary integrating force.

It is also heartening to see how, in spite of the difficulties, our own Playback Theatre idea has taken hold; how individuals from many different disciplines have found in it a source for which they were thirsting; how groups have summoned the commitment to offer their art to the unheralded and ignored, and not once, but through the years; how it spans culture and language, to the extent that Playback is now actively practiced in over 15 countries. We have been succeeding long enough to feel that we are building a tradition that has value. There have been a number of instances when our children, now adults, have come back to learn this tradition. To have built a way, a practice that can span the generations is truly heartwarming.

## SPONTANEITY AND SERVICE

In the course of this book, we have examined the connection of nonscripted theatre to oral culture and compared it to its antecedents in preliterary culture. We have explored NST with regard to actors, directors, and that special intermediary role, the conductor. We have discussed NST in rehearsal and in performance. We have touched on the company life of an NST group. In the process, we have elaborated certain key concepts.

The first is *spontaneity*, that flow of sensory information, evaluation, and action that fuels our ability to adapt with creativity to a constantly changing environment. Our lives consist of a mammoth improvisation, in which each moment is a spontaneity test. Do we take in the information? Can we make sense of it? Can we act on what we know? Improvisational theatre is both a practicing ground for life and an arena for exploring further dimensions of mind. Through it we can leap beyond the limitations of our frames of living, risking chaos to find order, daring not to think to achieve higher understanding, attempting to be both in the moment--so that our inhibitory observer is silenced-and *out* of it--benefitting from metalevel perspective. In one sense, it seems there is always a trade-off in dealing with these oppositions. It seems we cannot be equally thoughtful and sensual at the same time, or childish and adult, or sensitive to both Mood and Program needs. The fascination of spontaneity, however, is mastering just this trick. It seems magical when it happens and is a touchstone of human creativity. For it seems that we *can* achieve the impossible if we are only willing to keep trying. Spontaneity means mind and heart and body surpassing the boundaries of each. Spontaneity means unifying opposition. Spontaneity is play, is striving, is leaping beyond.

We have also emphasized a concept of *theatre* in these pages. It is delimited by consciousness on one end and timeless, mindless characteristics on the other. It involves enchantment, live communal encounter, atmosphere. It involves story, role taking, performance. It values improvisation and the flexibility to make drama out of the most here-and-now concerns.

Theatre can be a strong force for preserving social ecology, the “web of moral understandings and commitments that tie people together."[5] My own experience tells me this. For despite Playback Theatre's relative anonymity, its failure to obtain dependable funding, and even its inability to find a consistent public, I know that by doing theatre we have transformed individual lives and made aggregations of people into communities of memory. I remember, for example, the weekend workshop in which a Vietnam veteran told of the senseless battle and the injuries from which he would never fully recover. It was followed by the story of an underground resister, also present, also scarred by bullets, and the story of a woman whose man came back from the war, but was lost to her forever. None of us there will forget those stories, so central for our epoch. I remember a pair we did during a performance in a small town near the Hudson River. It was about the terror and the beauty of the river. It so happened that two boys had recently been drowned in it. I sensed, as we enacted “river beauty” and “river terror,” that the story of that community was being encapsulated in that moment. The stories have come in many shapes and colors. I remember a story about seeing a deer in the forest, about a wedding of five year-olds, about a dream of belonging to a traveling theatre troupe. Acting them out may be an exercise in fantasy, but it is a “compelling illusion,” to use Ernest Becker's phrase, which is finally more worthwhile than many of our other coping mechanisms. It is more sustaining than *People* magazine, unquestionably better than nuclear armaments, and arguably more relevant than church.

[5] Bellah et. al., 335.

Insofar as modern society does not cotton to improvisa- tional modes and drama events, these concepts of spontaneity and theatre also take on the force of ideals, which I propound for the improvement of society and the safety of the world. I would also like to cite two others.

The first is the ideal of *service*. My grandfather, who was Jewish, was a civil servant, dead set against my father's ambition to join the theatre. My father, being young and enterprising, had his way. But after a number of years, his father's community-minded argument, buttressed, I imagine, by a tradition of ethical responsibility, turned convincing. The theatre my father knew was in many ways self-indulgent, proud, hierarchical. He quit, turning to a more socially constructive career, and theatre's reputation, if it were possible, fell yet another notch.

The kind of theatre talked about in this book is an answer to my grandfather and father both. I believe theatre can be directly concerned with social needs. Moreover, I do not be- lieve that social workers, teachers, nurses, and clergy have a premium on helping others, insofar as most of them have tenure, pensions, and unemployment benefits. There are some, however, who serve without security. Many theatre people--especially NST theatre people--fit this category. Them I especially admire.

One of my favorite performances was for a group of Head Start teachers (Head Start is an inner-city prekindergarten program). They wanted us to come and perform on their staff development day, but they could pay no more than a small part of our fee. They were meeting in a church basement, a small, cluttered room, most unenterprising for our purposes. However, it was their world, and we entered it gladly. Their need for us was great, for their task was overwhelming--many of the children they taught, and their parents, faced tremendous problems--and the support the teachers received, in salary, work conditions, and training, was minimal. While the teachers sat on kindergarten chairs, we acted out their stories. They laughed till they cried. And they simply cried. Afterwards I was euphoric. I felt, “This is what we are meant for!” And all the while our support was no more munificent or long-lasting than theirs.

Such experiences confirm my belief in a citizen actor, who performs as needed by the community, then melts back into the social fabric-a modern answer to the aboriginals “of high degree" who live as ordinary tribespeople except when they are needed.

Even greater than the humility needed for such a concept is the courage of the true healer. Life-yours, mine-is difficult enough, but to voluntarily absorb the pain and problems of others, face the challenge of their dilemmas, seek to guide them toward new visions--this takes a particular kind of commitment.

Service without security, without fanfare, without adulation. 

“Communion is more than anything else a gift,” writes Gustavo Guttierrez. So in giving, we are given to, and we end up with riches of the sort that fill the pockets of the soul. This brings me to my final point of all--the importance of *grace*. One of my teachers explained what grace is by telling us this story: he had read in the paper of a young couple who had been swimming in the Pacific Ocean. A shark attacked the man. The woman dragged him, barely alive, as far as the shore, where he died. As, shocked and exhausted, she witnessed his last breaths, the woman reached her hand into the sea and in a ritual way sprinkled her lover with water. This, said my teacher, was *grace*.[6]

[6] Guttierez, 205. The teller of this story was Robert Fitzgerald, during a Harvard seminar on the epic in 1964.

There is something in the concept of grace that does not seek for perfection so much as find the perfect in what is. Non- scripted theatre will always be somewhat rough-hewn; a theatre of service will always be pulled down by the grubby realities of everyday life (the hall will be cold, the crowd unruly, the host frazzled); as performers we will always fail, trapped by our own anxieties and imperfections. It is not understanding alone that can help us cope with these imperfections--or better organization or even higher performance standards. We must also be- lieve in grace.

I am thinking of one more story. It takes place at a commu- nity center, where a teen theatre company has just performed. Their model was a familiar one: to present a skit based on teen problems, then invite the audience to query the actors, first in role, then as themselves. Attentive during the skit, the audience became especially involved during the question period. There was a great deal of spirited talking. In the final segment, a rather paunchy white-haired man stood up. In a German accent, he complained that even though the presentation was excellent, it overemphasized problems.

There was an immediate onslaught of disagreement, both from the troupe and from members of the audience. These things need to be faced, they said. It is very beneficial, they said. The answers will follow.

It turned out that the man was the center's consultant psychiatrist, perhaps an unlikely source for criticism of this sort. After all, a mental health worker should want problems to be aired, and a psychiatrist should be sensitive to the value of process and not demand solutions. Nevertheless, the man stood his ground, unbowed before the boisterous opposition to his position.

But I found myself agreeing with him, for reasons which only later came clear. What I had noticed at the time was that in many ways the presentation had not been very theatrical. There was no music, for example, nor theatrical lighting. The skit itself was only minimally fictive, with scant humor or playfulness. In short, while there had been truth in the teen show, it had no grace.

As I have thought about this occasion, I imagine that the psychiatrist was a European immigrant, Jewish, perhaps even a Holocaust survivor. I imagine that he had learned that what was important for him was not to *see* human degradation, but to *transcend* it, and in the teen theatre group's approach, he saw too much pain without the presence of redemption. This is what stories can do, this is what art does: describe the most difficult truths in a way that we can bear to remember because the rendition is beautiful. It is the ultimate triumph of our integrating mind. Life may be brutal, but we can enclose it in our poetry, our music, our play.

For me, what is most important is to create a theatre that is neither sentimental nor demonic, hermetic nor confrontational, but ultimately a theatre of love.
