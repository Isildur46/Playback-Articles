# CHAPTER TWELVE REHEARSING

Rehearsal for improvisation seems a contradiction in terms. How can one prepare if there is nothing prepared, how can one plan the unplanned? Even forgetting about the built-in unpredictability of improvisation, a perfectly spoken, highly polished product may well not even be an objective of an NST theatrical presentation.

In experimental theatre, rehearsal is a duty word, suggesting the dispirited practice of the literary theatre.NST groups much prefer the word “workshop.” Extensive workshopping is considered essential for the intense personal work and ensemble-building manyNST directors demand. “Rehearsal,” writes Schechner, is merely “aimed at one set of gestures relevant to one production,” while the workshop is aimed at “the development of the company." AndrÃé Gregory spent a year working with the actors of the Manhattan Project. The result, a production of “Alice in Wonderland,” was but the end part, not the end all, of the process. There even emerged, as part of this trend away from rehearsal for production, a new genre called“paratheatrics”which emphasized the workshop experience as an end in itself. The emergence of the interna- tional theatre institutes, such as Brook's and Eugenio Barba's, also reflect a commitment to long-term exploration and development unfettered by the traditional concept of theatre rehearsal. Although his terms are somewhat confusing, Augusto Boal takes the concept even further, finding particular value not only in the leisureliness of the workshop idea but in its informality. Consequently, he calls for a “rehearsal-theatre”instead of a “spectacle-theatre."[1]

[1] See Schechner 1973, 175; Arbus & Avedon, 79; Boal, I42.

In some of the other branches of NST, rehearsal has not been so much an issue as performance itself. But we will ex- amine that question in the next chapter.

A group's need to rehearse correlates to its place on the
NST-literary theatre spectrum. If gestures and/or words are fixed beforehand, for instance, then obviously rehearsal will be needed. In Playback Theatre, we customarily rehearse nothing more than our entrance, and this only when we are choosing something consciously theatrical-a matter of twenty minutes' work at the outside. In fact, for the most part, we do not rehearse at all.[2]

[2] Keith Johnstone writes about his foreshortened production process, in which the piece is being written as it is being rehearsed: “I don't see that the plays created in this way are inferior to those I struggle over, sometimes for years." (Johnstone, 28)

When Ron Jenkins was with his Balinese Topeng troupe, he observed that the rehearsals were “an excuse for gossip, drinking, and generally having a good time.” He is at pains to point out that these performers did not lack serious purpose. Instead, he suggests that for them, practicing lines or moves was unnecessary, even perhaps counterproductive. What was important was developing the right climate of communication; thus the emphasis was on the Mood, not the Program. After months of study, Jenkins was allowed to join the dancers in performance. Just before his last turn with them, he suggested something he would like to add to the show, and the others agreed. Jenkins performed his bit-a clown's comment on his experience living in the village--and it went over well. Such last-minute additions would be unthinkable in the literary theatre--especially as suggested by a minor player--but it is directly part of the process and value system of the oral tradition, which allows for, and is in fact based on, spontaneous elements. Jenkins's experience is corroborated by de Zoete, co-author of Dance and Drama in Bali, who comments that the performers often decided on what story they would enact just minutes before show time.[3]

[3] Jenkins, 55; de Zoete & Spies, 39.

In Playback we operate the same way. We do not decide on our stories at all, of course, but wait for them to come from the audience. However, we will usually decide during our pre- performance warm-up just how we will enter or make use of an unusual element.

It has been a common question from the public, amazed at the clearly unplanned nature of the Playback actors'work, to wonder what the company does in its “rehearsals." I usually reply, “We go to the diner.” Actually, we often do, in the best Balinese tradition.

## REHEARSAL OBJECTIVES

Nevertheless, there is a great deal to occupy the time of NST practitioners in their nonrehearsals, which from now on I will call “practices.” Our Playback practices have focused on three main areas: maintaining open lines of communication between company members, developing the form, and improving acting skills.

In spontaneous theatre, which by definition requires an open channel between performers' Program and their Mood, regular and skilled attention to the actors'well-being is imperative. I have stated my belief that the leaders of such groups need to be trained in dealing with the vicissitudes of mood among their charges. Furthermore, such maintenance takes time, time spent in personal talking rather than in “doing theatre." This can be hard both for directors, feeling the pressure of performance schedules, and actors, whose bodies prefer constant action.

It is very important to honor the needs of what I am calling “the form," whether it be Playback Theatre, Family Life Theatre, the Dorothy Heathcote method, the Theatre Games method, or whatever. The fact is, these approaches each have their own particular set of goals and objectives (albeit within the general NST framework). Thus attention to the needs of the form will lead to rich areas of focus for groups. For example, when we started doing Playback Theatre we just did scenes.  We had no music, lights, or props. Fluid sculptures evolved later, then the sound sculptures, the pairs--many ways to enact a persons's story. Music became very important.  We added lights and colored pieces of cloth for use as props. All this improved the form and required new kinds of practice. We had to become skilled in our new ways of story enactment; spend hours practicing with the music; experiment extensively with the cloth. It turned out to be very difficult to maintain a strong, positive demeanor on stage in between fictive roles; so we paid a great deal of attention to our personal presence. We also came to appreciate the segment we call the setting-up, which takes place between the teller's interview and the enactment of the scene. The setting-up involves the actor in an intermediary stage between the everyday self and the fictive role, which, if fulfilled well, has dramatic power of its own: music plays, the lights change, and actors move slowly and as if propelled by a larger force to the prop tree, to their places, where they wait, immobile, until all movement has ceased and it is time for the action to begin. So we worked on this special kind of presence. We also worked on our presence in-between scenes, during that period when a new teller comes forward and is interviewed, and the actors sit on the boxes as themselves. Such work had little to do with acting as we understood the term. But “acting properly” at these times was demanded by our form.

In addition, we spent months experimenting with inno- vations which were later dropped because they did not seem to suit the form.

The only acting skill needed for the literary theatre not necessary for work in the NST might be script-reading, al- though the sense of story acquired through reading is invaluable for a nonscripted actor. In all theatre, actors have but voice and body; the more expressive, the better. Of course, emphasis and point of concentration will differ. In general, however, there is a place in the nonscripted theatre for good acting.

It is also true that the NST allows for what I call “informal theatre," such as Playback's audience-up segment, where a skilled production is traded for a sort of raw spontaneity. Take these examples:

In a day-long stress reduction and spontaneity workshop, company employees are divided into teams and given thirty minutes to plan and rehearse a skit on “the future of their company." The results are likely to be more creative than if they are given an hour, or a day.

A teenage acting company, comprised of residents of a shelter for runaways, presents skits about life experiences without costumes, props, or lights. Their preparation is minimal. Yet the presentations possess a tremendous dramatic power because of their authenticity. It is hard to imagine their skits improving with more rehearsal. The degree of formal and informal aspects requires careful balance, and is another conceptual frame important for the nonscripted theatre.

I love the informal theatre--the theatre of little children, family charades, small-town pageants-theatre which does not worry about the art as much as the *essence* of a dramatic experience. While I would never contend that informality has any claim over an elaborately rehearsed and presented production, once a threshold of perfectibility is crossed, an immense artistic distance needs to be traveled to accomplish an equivalent dramatic impact. In this sense the choice between NST and the literary theatre is how one wishes to spend one's time. A conception which is not clear on this question may well fall between stools, resulting in a production whose aim is too high for its talent and resources to sustain--an all-too- common occurrence. (One also sees the opposite: a totally rehearsed, slick production pretending to be informal and genuinely spontaneous, even though--in live situations, at least--audiences are rarely fooled.)

## PRACTICE PROCEDURES

A final observation concerns how to go about conducting NST practices. We have discussed the importance of workshop time devoted to good communication, developing the form, and improving skills. We have also seen how rehearsal is sometimes necessary, such as for an entrance, a song, or a set piece. There is always much to do, and especially sinceNST groups are usually set up along less than fully professional lines, practices will be relatively few. Such a situation increases the pressure for a Programmatic bias to the practice session. Our emphasis on spontaneity, however, would suggest that the structure of practices--like the performances--must strive for a balance of Mood and Program.

In the experimental theatre there has been an increasing emphasis on a highly disciplined approach. Grotowski, impressed by certain Asian yogic models, has influenced many groups to begin each session with intensive psychophysical exercises which both warm up the actor to physical tasks and create an atmosphere of solemnity.

The Grotowski method utilizes ritual exercises to rid the actors of here-and-now interferences. The spontaneity method, on the other hand, recognizes here-and-now thoughts and feelings as potential creative elements and attempts to use them.

Thus the spontaneity “method” is to some extent no method--i.e., there is no way to do it the same way every time. Each practice, and each moment of each practice, constitutes a challenge to the company to find the right balance, the right activity, the right feeling.

In our Playback practices, we most often start with physical and vocal warm-ups (*Program*). We also do some sort of interpersonal connecting (*Mood*), since we may have been apart for a week. Which element gets the most emphasis will depend on the circumstance. Question: Who decides, and how?

To be sure, there are certain exercises that we often, even regularly, undertake. But we do none of them religiously, for we seek surprise, variation, creative innovation, play. From practice to practice, performance to performance, the mix will change. Sometimes it will be best to begin with talking; at other times, a rigorous warm-up to acting. Often, we are silly. Sometimes, we simply dance, neither verbally checking-in nor doing physical exercise, but in the process accomplishing the objectives of both.

I am wary of directors who have too strict a method, who cannot engage in the play of opposite notions.

Audience well-wishers often tell us afterwards that we have improved so much since they saw us last, and we are genuinely surprised, since our practices, with their constant shifts of focus, have usually felt so flighty and unproductive. Yet they are right.

Spontaneity is an elusive and often frustrating quality. It takes a high degree of personal discipline, and many of us often yearn for a more automatic order. Moreover, this discipline must be shared by the group as a whole, a collective responsibility for maximizing the creative potential of each moment of practicing together. In such circumstances, there can be remarkable progress of a Program nature--despite, no, because of--the dancing, the interpersonal dealing, and the trips to the diner.
