# INTRODUCTION FOUR ACTORS, NO AUDIENCE

It is a Sunday afternoon in winter, the early afternoon sky bright despite a cover of cloud. The light pours in through the big windows of the church hall, in the centre of which are placed about thirty chairs facing a line of plastic milk crates. On one side of the crates sits a collection of musical instruments. On the other side brightly colored cloth hangs from a wooden prop tree. The voices of children resound in the hall as their parents usher them in and help them off with coats. In a back room I am with my actors. We are facing our first performance with a new company and a new approach called Playback Theatre. It is totally improvisational. Our objective is to act out, using mime, music, as well as spoken scenes, the personal stories of the audience. 

I am holding up a large piece of newsprint on which I have written the “Order of Events.” We have our entrance, our introductions, then fluid sculptures, stories, the “workshop phase," more stories, and a finale defined only as “something at the end." In fear and excitement, we grasp hands, line up, and enter the hall.

What began in that hall is an adventure that continues. My two outstanding memories of that day are first, that it worked; the audience, predominantly family and friends, was pleased, and the actors felt inspired to carry on to our next shows, on the children's ward of a general hospital and at a university conference on social value and the arts. 

The second memory is of a 6-year old boy who told his dream. The dream was a fearful one. The boy, it so happened, was the son of one of the actors. She had recently separated from her husband, and the unsettledness of the family was clearly reflected in the content of the dream. I was aware of the complexity of communication that was taking place as the boy told his story and the actors acted it out, with his mother, sister and grandmother watching.

There is something very informal about this Playback Theatre. We invite people to tell anything. We often even invite them to come to the stage and be actors for a story.
There is a lot of conversation back and forth between audience and actors. It is so loose and playful sometimes that I have described it as “one step beyond charades.”

And yet there is clearly the potential for great seriousness, and even emotional upheaval. I sensed it in the case of that young boy as the actors dramatized his nightmare. I remember asking him if he would like to see it turn out differently. I said we could act out that version on the spot, too (what we came later to term a “transformation"). But he said, No. It was enough for him to tell it the way it was (with his intimates looking on, and the rest of us bearing witness to his fear).

That first performance took place in 1975. Prior to then, I had been caught up in the excitement of the experimental theatre and avant-garde forms of art. But what I had studied at university were the ancient epics, those pre-literary tales from (what were for me) incredibly far away times and places. Later I spent two years living in a tiny, totally pre-industrial village in Nepal. In my eyes this new Playback Theatre, in which the people could see their stories acted out and share with their neighbors aspects of their daily lives and deeply felt concerns, was a way to bring an ancient way into the present, except that where traditional societies sang of Gods and Heroes known and revered by the entire clan, we would sing about ourselves, in all our ordinariness. My hope and deep conviction was that in this process we would feel elevated, as if in contact with “the Gods,” and at the end a disparate community would cohere.

We followed certain principles. For example, I told my actors that I never wanted them to do this work more than half time, because I did not want them to share the fate of many modern actors, forced to become exotic, hothouse flowers, with brilliant colors and severe pruning; instead I wanted them to live in the world and be like their audiences, men and women of common work, family responsibilities, and civic duty.

In many ways we were groping for a new synthesis of old and contemporary. We needed to forge a terminology.  What would we call this kind of theatre. Life-theatre? Impromptu life theatre? We ended up with *Playback Theatre*, which connoted “playing back” the stories of audience members. What should we call the person who came up from the audience to a special chair in order to speak? Narrator? Hero? Revealer? We chose “teller.” What to call the emcee, who sat next to the teller and interfaced between audience and actors? Impresario? Guide? Magus? We chose“conductor,”suggestive of conducting energy as well as directing the concert. Finally, what about those who watched and listened? Should we call them the group, the flock, the people? We chose in the end the most familiar, “audience.”

Over ten years later, not long before I started on this manuscript, this vocabulary had become everyday for us and for many others who had become involved in the Playback Theatre way of sharing stories. We were still going strong. In fact six of the actors from that beginning phase were still members of the Company. We would soon have to begin to refer to ourselves as the original company, because in other cities and towns Playback Theatre groups would form to enact the stories of their communities. Looking back over those pieces of newsprint, now browning with age, that I held up to actors before we went on, original items, such as fluid sculptures and scenes (or stories), continued to be listed, while others, including “ditty,” “walkabout,” and even “walklava,” were dropped. Our mission was to find effective dramatic forms for the enactment of any and all personal stories. We tried many different ways, some dance-like, others clowny, others psychodramatic. Over time we learned that our form demanded its own aesthetic. Some of our experiments suited the Playback approach and became part of our dramatic tradition, while others, after a hot period of discovery, turned out to be less effective and were eventually dropped. The postperformance notes I wrote on the newsprint pages reflect the concerns of an ongoing theatre company; “so and so absent;" “so and so in crisis.” “Fifty (audience size);” “twenty- two;” “four actors, no audience;” “low key;” “voices soft;” “excellent.” The places: “Bellevue Hospital,” Chicago,” “Mount St. Mary College,” “FF”(First Friday, our box office performanceseries); Woodstock,” “Heart Association,” “Westbeth Theatre” (New York City)，“Secretaries,”
“A.S.G.P.P.”(PsychodramaAssociation), “National Ski Pa- trollers.”

After hundreds of performances and workshops, we still felt fear and excitement before the start. After all, we had no script to rely on. On every occasion we felt the challenge of providing a stimulating experience, and also living up to an ideal that said that this very challenge of finding meaning was not ours alone, but a collective search to be shared by all who were in the room. Of course, we were not so naive anymore. We had developed our skills and a sense of professionalism about our craft. Afterwards we often felt dissatisfied, even while being surrounded by excited and grateful audience members. Certain actors often felt emotionally drained afterwards, particularly if the stories had been heavy, and their roles difficult. This was the fatigue of the latter-day shaman after the exorcism. We needed our cooling-down, and we felt at such moments the challenge of our service.

Fortunately, we as often felt incredibly enriched.

Then there were the questions, often well meaning, sometimes harsh. “What is all this for, anyway?” “Is this theatre, or therapy?” “How *often* do you practice?” “*How* do you practice?” “Are you professional?” It was not always easy to find a satisfactory response.

In many ways Playback Theatre does not fit into the standard mold for theatre, and our company does not fit into a conventional shape. There was no title for the show. We did not enact plays. The actors had other jobs, some full time. We only rehearsed once a week. In our practices we spent almost as much time on group process as on skill building. We had long debates among ourselves about the ultimate purpose of our work, but felt, after all was said and done, yes, it was therapeutic, and yes, it was theatre. The most important point was that it worked and seemed to quench a thirst.

Playback Theatre is not the only type of contemporary, interactive, nonscripted theatre. In this book my purpose is to offer a framework for understanding Playback Theatre and similar approaches. I will write about nonliterary culture, because what we are recreating might be called a postliterary theatre that has affinity with ancient ways. I will answer those difficult questions by explaining the nature and the purpose of nonscripted theatre. I will discuss the actor, both in practice and performance. I will discuss the role of the intermediary, in our term, the conductor. In the process, I will write about some of the crucial issues for non-scripted performers--spontaneity, safety, ensemble, and artistic standards. My purpose is to provide a workable rationale for an improvisational, healing theatre suitable for today's world. I will argue that yes, it is possible to create *theatre* without scripts; theatre, moreover, that can be a powerful force for social transformation.

I want to tell you about the time when my notes said, “four actors, no audience.” We had been invited to take part in a program sponsored by a county arts council for outreach per- formances in small towns. The entire morning leading up to this matinee, I had been dealing with actors who could not perform in the show, either because of other commitments or personal crises. In short, it wasn't shaping up to be one of our best days. The six of us who could make it (four actors, conductor and musician)arrived at the theatre chosen for the performance venue only to find that there had been a mistake, and no one was expecting us. In fact, another group was there in the midst of a rehearsal. Our performance was switched to the children's home on the hill, one of the only other places in town with a large space. We were told that the audience would be sent on. We duly went up to the children's home, set up our boxes, cloth, and instruments, and waited. Not surprisingly, no one came. After a while I asked the Sister in charge if there might be some children who would like to attend. She responded eagerly and soon we had an audience of eight kids, plus attendants. There were also three or four of our friends and relatives. The children were highly impaired, but we did not hesitate. We-the children, the caretakers, the actors, the relatives of the actors-spent the next couple of hours acting out stories, singing, and parading. The children were so innocent, so open, so appreciative of us grownups playing for them, and what they told was magical. By the time it was over, we couldn't have imagined a more satisfying afternoon.

Our ending up in that children's home that afternoon was an accident, but only barely. For since that first performance in the church hall, our interest has been to enact the stories of every kind of person in every kind of setting. There are many people, perhaps like the children segregated in that home, who are not always listened to. Even more numerous are the people whose stories in one way or another are too terrible for others to want to hear. If oppressed persons can be defined as those who have nowhere to tell their story, our mission has been to provide a space for anyone and everyone to be heard. There is a spiritual aspect to such a calling. This is not a question of proclaiming a manifesto. It has to be lived, and it is living in this way that I wish to write about--actors living in service to their neighbors, and in the process, to themselves. [1]
