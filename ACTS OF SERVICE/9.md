Thetendency in the literary theatre is to typecast actors according to looks and outstanding prior roles. The NST, with its emphasis on improvisation and ensemble, is different.NST actors are asked to play all sort of roles, from heroines to dogs, from God to a Mack truck. Thus the NST actor needs not only a basic expressiveness, but a high degree of role flexibility. It is not enough to be outstanding in one role, or one kind of role. He or she must have the spontaneity to play many roles quickly. In Playback, for example, an actor may play a tragic role and minutes later be chosen for something totally different (thus clean entry and exit from roles is an area we intensively work on). Even within a Playback scene, an extended ending called a “transformation," in which the scene is redone according to the teller's ideal image, may require an actor to portray immediately an opposite characterization--from defeat, say, to triumph. NST directors and teachers devise exercises to help actors develop the necessary speed and flexibility of role transition.[1]

Also see Transformation and Inside-Outside exercises from Joseph Chaikin in Pasolli and Character Agility and Transforming Relationship exercises in Spolin, who asserts that “transforming, whether it be of character, object, or idea, seems to be essentially what must take place within every scene of improvisation." (page 272)

Nevertheless, because human beings crave regularity as much as they crave novelty, there is a strong role repetition in the NST, too. Sometimes the desire for immediate audience success will cause a group to solidify a dynamic combination, even though opting for role flexibility holds long-range promise for a more creative ensemble. Thus, a Mental Health Players group, which assigns the roles before a presentation, will often assign Arnie the alcoholic husband's role again and again. He does it so naturally, and switching around requires such effort. Shelley Berman, a member of the Compass Company,speaks of his frustration when he was made to perform alone because his monologues were a hit. He is vehement on the topic of the improvisation groups which “find several pieces, canonize them, then sell themselves as a revue: You act like it's an improvisation when the fact is you know goddamn well what your tricks are going to be..."Once you cease taking chances, you've lost your craft."[2] The principle of flexibility is easy, but the practice is hard, even when conscientiously pursued, since some individuals and some combinations seem to fit so perfectly together.

[2] Sweet, 134.

But this is not all. In theNST, since the language is not pre- cooked, actors must be playwrights, too. To be a playwright requires a sense of form and a linguisticinventiveness, an intelligence, in fact, which comes from the ability to stand apart from experience. Much of the literary actors'task is to put themselves as fully as possible in the fictive moment, whereas the NST actor, while needing to be in just as fully, must also understand and comment on the scene by inventing it as it happens. In other words, the NST actor needs a particularly high level of spontaneity. Thus plasticity and emotional lability in an actor is not enough for NST; we must also include in the criteria the narrative skills of a storymaker.[3]


[3] At the Drama Action Centre in Sydney, a major training school for NST approaches, being a “playwright” has been stressed as a key curriculum concept. See Batten.

Furthermore,the NST actor needs another capacity not necessary for the literary theatre--to be able to be on stage as him or herself. NST's predilection for shifting back and forth between everyday and fictive levels means that the actor must not only be a role player, but a person appearing in a public ceremony. There is much dramatic potential in the actor's willingness to show himself or herself as a fellow human being, but it takes a certain level of self-confidence and other- directedness. For many actors, this aspect is enough to keep them away from the NST altogether.

Thus, in considering actors for our Company, we do not look at an individual for a particular role, but for all roles- flexibility is key. Beyond that, however, we also assess the actor as playwright. There is another major consideration as well the ability to be a good group member; we will discuss that factor in the chapter on company life.

RISK AND SAFETY

If Otto Rank is correct in saying that a preponderance of the *fear of life* leads to neurotic repression, while *fear of death* leads to production, then a distaste for improvisation can be seen as the former, and a preference for plays, the latter.[4]  But a life of no risk can lead to stagnation, as many middle-aged people discover when the basic ingredients of adult life- establishing a career, marriage, children--are no longer new and terrifying. The fact is, we felt alive in those days of youthful adventure, and it seems that risk is so basic to our sense of well-being that if we cannot find a good new challenge, we are liable to turn to mischief--cheating on our spouse, employer, or ourselves (drinking too much, eating too much).

[4] Rank, 148.

When the idea for Playback Theatre came to me, I knew that it was important for me. Within a year I had formed a theatre company devoted to developing the idea. I remember how my stomach churned on the way to practices in the first year. I was so afraid, I wasn't sure I could stand it. When we first started performing, we were fierce--both in terror and excitement. We rode on waves of exhilaration. Our sense of adventure led us to try all sorts of ideas and succeed with many of them. Eventually, those feelings diminished, to be replaced by a sense of having done it before. My fear, our fear, passed. So did our excitement. Recently, despite our allegiance to each other and to the idea, we faced up to our sense of fatigue. We knew that if we were to continue to be spontaneous, we needed change. So we did what for us was a most difficult thing-we stopped regular rehearsing and performing. We allowed the group to go into semi-retirement, so that individuals could initiate new, independent activities.

A sense of risk is essential to creativity, but surely there are occasions when risk is to be avoided, when self-protection may be right. What about *bad* risks? Once at a residential workshop a few of us were fooling around at the end of the evening. We started doing Playback, but the goal was to violate, not honor the teller. The scenes were hilarious and incredibly energetic, as we released tension from the burden of so much hard, constructive work during the day. Many people were involved, including myself, and the scenes got wilder, until finally, exhausted, we all went off to bed. But that was not to be the end of the story. Some people had been deeply hurt by what most of us saw as ‘play' and it took a long time to clear up. Afterwards, several participants said they had felt very anxious about what was happening, but had gone along anyway because everyone else was.

I have thus learned first hand of the danger of such irreverent urges. Any theatre company with a strong commitment to liminality has undoubtedly experienced similar dan- gers.  The power of the liminal is so great that it cannot be played with lightly. True, I praised irreverence earlier, and seem to be condemning it here. In the first instance, however, ir-| reverence and childishness were part of a warming-up stage for serious improvisational acting, whereas in the second, irreverence and Dionysian excess took place without a framework of purpose or understanding.

In my work I make a Rankian distinction between anxiety, which shuts down our spontaneous capacities, and fear, which releases them. Anxiety is a valid warning sign not to do something, while fear is a sign that what one is about to do has value and to go ahead. Knowing in the moment whether that sinking sensation is anxiety or fear requires quite a high level of self-knowledge; it is essential knowledge for actors who truly want to be agents of transformation.

Actors, however, are not known for their strength of personality. Some are seduced into the profession in search of fame or wealth; others are weak when it comes to autonomous interpersonal relations. These are the individuals in danger of taking bad risks.

The concept of emotion memory, for example, has always made me nervous--not because I object to the basic idea of drawing on personal experience as a source of affect, but because I fear that in the product-oriented modern theatre, insufficient attention is paid to maintaining a creative balance between the personal and the professional. In the process, many actors, swayed by the system, sacrifice their personhood.  In some cultures it is felt that to say out loud a person's name vitiates basic life force, and so he or she is “named" only with great consideration. In the same way, I feel that the sense of our own story is one of our most precious possessions, which can be squandered and “named”promiscuously. For example, an actress who had come to a Playback workshop told me of an audition in which she was asked to stand up and tell a joke. When she had trouble, the (woman) director asked her if she had ever been sexually assaulted. She had been raped, she an- swered. Then tell me about it as a joke, said the director. The actress complied, and her performance was full of emotional vitality. (She did not get the part.) A friend who is an actor told me once of an experimental production in which a character slapped him in the face until he cried. This happened every night, and he said that the only way he could cry was to reach a point where he believed his fellow actor really meant it. Such acting strikes me as masochistic.

Lee Strasberg has said that the “basic art of acting is a monstrous thing because...the body with which you make real love is the same body with which you make fictitious love with someone you don't like, whom you fight with, whom you hate, by whom you hate to be touched." This is a provocative statement. What can be said for it is that at least Strasberg implies the importance of knowing the difference between art and life, even if they are at cross-purposes.

The capacity for “as if,” which is the basis of all theatre, has been considered in a negative light in psychology. For example, when Helene Deutsch referred to the “as if”personality type, she meant an individual existing on the borderline between psychosis and neurosis. A more recent exponent of the same position is Murray Bowen, whose concept of the “pseudo self” suggests an emotion-driven conformist eager to“pretend”in order to gain acceptance from others. “The pseudo-self is an actor and can be many different selfs [*sic*]. The list of pretends is extensive," writes Bowen. The contrast is the “solid self” individual, who does not participate in the “fusion" phenomenon: “The solid self says, ‘This is who I am, what I believe, what I stand for, and what I will do or will not do' in a given situation."[5]

The spontaneous person--and this includes actors--will enter into a context of pretend from a solid self position, to use Murray's term. He or she will be able to act appropriately, adjusting frames of understanding with flexibility.

That actors suffer psychologically from being professional “as if'ers” is undeniable. That they often commit unsettling acts is usually of little concern to producers and directors, interested in financial and/or artistic success; or audiences, totally estranged from any knowledge of the actors as fellow human beings. Personally, however, I have always felt that theatre must also be for the good of the actors. It will only be so if actors themselves, as well as their employers, value the right balance between personal and fictive roles. Let the “theatre dangerous”be a “theatre of liminality", not one of gladiatorial immolation.

What I am saying, then, is that when the actor is afraid, that is a green light. When the actor is anxious, that is a yellow light.

That fear is positive and anxiety negative...of course, the subject is not so simple. Paul Tillich argues that anxiety is an inevitable part of being human. Moreover it is precisely when we lack the courage to face our ontological insecurity that we retreat into “nonbeing,” which often results in a need for
Lcertitude. Thus a kind of deep-level anxiety may not be a sign of something wrong so much as proof of our openness to the conundrum of existence. It seems to me that in the theatre, as in other arenas, human beings find refuge in a language and gesture of affirmation which is too pat. It is a veneer. When such optimism conceals a sense of exploitation or misadventure, one had better stop and get out; when it masks apprehension about encountering the human condition, then bravery and continued effort are the only anodyne.
