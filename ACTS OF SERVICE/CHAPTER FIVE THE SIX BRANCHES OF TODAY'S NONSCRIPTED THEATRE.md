CHAPTER FIVE THE SIX BRANCHES OF TODAY'S NONSCRIPTED THEATRE

With a sense of the relation between the preliterary dramatic paradigm and Playback Theatre, I would now like to broaden the scope of discussion and explore, in this and the next chapters, the relation between preliterary enactments and other contemporary forms of nonscripted theatre (abbreviated below as“NST").

Let us survey the contemporary field with a quick eye. Dividing NST into six branches reflects the considerable scope and extent of activity in the domain of contemporary oral theatre. The categories are ranked roughly in order of the extent to which they are in the public eye.

First is *experimental theatre*, whose progenitor in the United
States is the Living Theatre, a collective led by Judith Malina and Julian Beck.[1] Antonin Artaud, Erwin Piscator, Bertolt
Brecht, and Jerzy Grotowski influenced the Living Theatre's development. Its heyday was during the period of political ferment and social change popularly referred to as “the sixties.” Some theatre companies, such as the Performance Group, the Open Theatre, the Manhattan Project, and Mabou Mines, sought a new aesthetic. Others were more concerned with political reform and called themselves “guerrilla”theatre, including the Bread and Puppet Theatre,the San Francisco Mime Troop, and El Teatro Camposino. Today the tremendous energy of the experimental theatre movement is considered spent, vitiated by the resurgence of social complacency, and replaced by Off-Off-Broadway and performance art.

[1] The following discussion will focus mostly on American theatre groups. Readers from other countries will need to compare these findings to their own cultural histories.

Second is the *educational theatre* movement, which had di- verse beginnings in the twenties, thirties, and forties in profes- sional children's theatre, recreation programs, sponsoring committees, and the universities. Educational theatre generally concerned itself either with theatre for education, or theatre as offered in schools. After the Second World War, a strong methodological focus emerged in England, with the work of Peter Slade, Brian Way, and Dorothy Heathcote. Considerable cross-fertilization has taken place on this side of the Atlantic. Since the seventies, another British import, the Theatre in Education (T.I.E.) concept, in which professional actors perform and conduct workshops with students, has had a major impact in America. An indigenous development of importance is the “theatre game" approach developed by Viola Spolin. Nevertheless, despite the established feeling of educational theatre as a field--by 1960 over 350 American universities offered courses in some sort of children's drama-educational theatre has not taken root where it counts most, among American children. Today, most American children experience theatre as an after- school extracurricular pursuit at the high school level, with very little formal exposure before then.

Third on the NST list is *comic-satiric theatre*, which had its genesis in the fifties in Chicago,when David Shepherd and
Paul Sills, son of theatre games originator Viola Spolin, founded a theatre company called Compass. Compass achieved notice by offering an evening of irreverent and often pithy improvisational skits. The group later became Second City, which achieved commercial success, replicated itself in a number of cities, and even inspired a new comedy wave on television embodied in “Saturday Night Live” in the seventies. A recent offshoot of this tradition is the franchised“Theatre Sports,” in which teams of actors play theatre games before an audience. 

Fourth is *community theatre*, which to many Americans connotes amateur groups in cities and towns, which despite their allegiance to literary traditions, nonetheless strongly embody many of the community values basic to nonscripted theatre. A special form of community theatre originated in the urban settlement houses of the 1920s, places such as Hull House in Chicago and the Henry Street Settlement in New York, where theatre was seen as a way of acculturating immi- grant tenants. One might also mention the W.P.A. Theatre Project, undertaken during the Depression; although it never espoused improvisation in performance, it nevertheless in- stituted such spontaneous activities as the“Living Newspaper” and had a populist, participatory ethos.

Today, community theatre, at least as conceived of by fund- ing bodies such as arts councils, tends to be subsumed under the rubric of “ethnic”theatre, emphasizing the performance of authentic native cultural traditions. It would appear that the
British “community arts”movement, consolidated around the principles of participation and process, has failed to find organized support in the United States.[2]

[2] Jane Addams comments with some eloquence on the important role of theatre in the cultural activities of the Hull-House settlement. She saw great benefit in the “effort to understand life through its dramatic portrayal, to see one's own participation intelligibly set forth,” and actively promoted both productions of European drama and plays written by immigrants in an effort to “formulate their situation in America." See Addams, 269.

Fifth is the *therapeutic theatre*, developed by psychiatrist J. L. Moreno, who founded psychodrama and its related applications, role playing and sociodrama.

As a young doctor in Vienna, Moreno directed a company called “Stegreiftheater” (*Theatre of Spontaneity*),eventually focussing on the real-life stories of his actors. Psychodrama has been actively practiced in the United States since the thirties, and Moreno's abiding interest in community applications has led to a therapeutic theatre movement which might well be categorized as community theatre. Examples include special populations dramatizing their own experience, such as prisoners (Cell Block Theatre, the Family), teenagers (Family Life Theatre, Acting Out Repertory), the disabled (National Theatre for the Deaf, Beyond Analysis), and the elderly (Elders Share the Arts). The’ Mental Health Players movement dramatizes community mental health issues using actors drawn from psychiatric hospital staff and the community at large.
Many applications of therapeutic theatre today would fall under the category of drama therapy, a growing field formally organized only in the last decade.[3]

[3] For more on Moreno's theatre work, see Fox 1978, 109-116; Fox 1987, 210-212; Fox 1993. For a description of contemporary therapeutic theatre written by two prominent figures in the drama therapy movement, see Emunah & Johnson, 233-239.

Finally we come to *clowning* and what has been called the
"new vaudeville." In the past decade there has been a turnabout in the American love affair with the three-ring circus. One-ring, European-style circuses, such as the Pickering Family Circus in San Franciscoand the Big Apple Circus in New York City, have valued an intimate, human relationship with audiences. Many of the new clowns are college graduates, children of the mass media age. Nevertheless, they have sought to ally themselves with a conspicuously nonliterary tradition and found a growing audience that supports them.

One might consider other examples of NST today--the Christmas pageants, the summer spectacles, the indigenous community dramas, as well as examples which might not be strictly termed “theatre” such as spontaneous, interactive radio programs.[4]

[4] One might include television talk shows. Generally speaking, however, they exploit the public's longing for live, constructive non-scripted drama rather than serve it.

THE CONTRAST: LITERARY THEATRE

In order to comment further on specific NST branches, let's pause for a moment and compare the preliterary drama paradigm with literary theatre:

First of all, it is important to recognize that modern theatre has not entirely lost touch with its oral roots. It still retains aspects of any or all of the paradigm's components. For example, the sculptured and elegant hall, the ritual of curtain-raising, the lighting, and the music can be considered trance-inducing elements which envelop the language of a play in spectacle. Another common way in which the literary theatre reaches towards the oral tradition is in the magic of its technical effects, which at its best, transports us beyond everyday reality.

Nevertheless, the literary theatre stands significantly apart from our preliterary model. First and foremost, it is a theatre of *language*; *written* words carry the essence of the meaning. While the events in the play are likely to be specific and the details concrete, the impact centers on an abstracted sense of focussed happening, the plot. A play does not follow a meandering, episodic course, adjusting to the exigencies of the environment; rather it bends at every turn to the demands of predetermined thought. This intellectual emphasis, in turn, produces a corresponding de-emphasis on extended physical activity and nonverbal components.

Where preliterary drama is intimate and inclusive, the literary theatre, which has developed along with the great urban civilizations, is a theatre of strangers--or has become increas- ingly so as Western civilization has evolved. The actors are not known personally to the members of the audience as is the case with preliterary drama. Furthermore, the literary actor's role is highly exclusive-the line between the audience and performers is inviolable. The very role of performer is itself open only to a select few who achieve membership in the performer group only after a highly competitive struggle. This process is the antithesis of an oral context in which, for example in Bali, one of the finest *gamelan* musicians is “a chauffeur who is also a composer of bewildering facility, and his principal collaborator is a boy about five."[5]

[5] de Zoete & Spies, 6.

The line between performers and audience is also a consequence of maintaining what is called aesthetic distance, which brings us to a third major distinction: the literary theatre is fundamentally aesthetic in purpose, not redressive. The very concept of art is a consequence of the literary mind, as we have already noted. Art means artifact, an aesthetic product, which in the always evanescent performing arts has come to mean an emphasis on performance as product. In such a context, the *process* matters mainly insofar as it enhances the product artistically and commercially. That the theatre “heal” anybody or anything is distinctly *not* an objective.

Finally, we can appreciate the extent to which literary theatre has been associated with a related kind of artifact, the “theatre" in which plays take place. Since Greek times, theatre has developed in association with innovative architecture and technical progress. We have gone from amphitheatre to roofed theatre, from lamplight to computerized electronic lighting systems, from open admission at the gate to telephoned credit card reservation and payment--in short from the open air to an artificial, highly engineered indoor milieu.

PLAYS OUT OF FAVOR

With a sense, then, of the major distinctions between pre- literary and literary theatre as outlined in the first chapters, let us now look again at NST. To what extent is the nonscripted theatre movement part of the oral tradition?

What strikes one immediatelyis NST's disaffection with the theatre of plays. “We don't need plays,” declared Julian Beck.  “We need action.” Many groups followed the Living Theatre in developing their own theme-based pieces which never included a text. An example was Joseph Chaikin, director of the Open Theater, who developed a “post-naturalistic” approach aimed at achieving the same freedom“abstract forms have provided in the other arts.”Grotowski's practice of reworking playtexts was widely followed, often resulting in a kind of hieratic language. Inspired by Artaud's interest in arousing a “hunger”as compelling as culture, many theatre practitioners developed an interest in a theatre of the senses, in contrast to a theatre where text was primary.[6]

[6] See Pasolli. That these experimental currents reached into the literary theatre as well is exemplified in Peter Weiss's *Marat/Sade*.
See Sontag in References.

In educational, community, and therapeutic theatre, the concern was not so much a rupture with cultural norms as an interest in the motivation of spectators, who were often encour- aged to be actors. Rather than wait for audience members to come to the box office, actors and teachers went out to them- to their classrooms, neighborhood centers, or hospitals. The method was thus not to impose a work from“outside”the group, but to develop dramatically the ideas and inspirations of the “participants” themselves. Viola Spolin, for example, considered“playwriting” a“misdemeanor,” a “manipulation of situation and fellow actors.” Moreover an actor's reference point was not dramatizing a playtext, but life itself. J.L. Moreno belonged to that segment of turn-of-the-century Viennese culture which espoused a deep suspicion of the effica- cy of language. The act, Moreno insisted, comes first.[7]

[7] See Spolin, 388; Fox 1987, xiii-xiv.

In the experimental theatre especially, the rejection of plays allowed for a physicality that was often quite breathtaking. Grotowski, influenced by nonWestern models of actor train- ing, imposed rigorous“psychophysical”exercises designed to develop untapped expressive powers of a distinctly nonlinguistic nature. One of Peter Brook's actors, Andreas Katsulas, expressed his attitude to acting this way: “Work to me is not being able to move at the end of the day.”[8]

[8] Heilpern, 277.

A singular characteristicof NST groups is their communal orientation. We have already noted a proclivity towards collective creation--company members as their own “authors," whether a group of adolescents in a runaway shelter, a class of fourth graders, or the Performance Group (who developed a piece called *Commune*). Political conviction and lifestyle choices entered into this keen interest in ensemble in the 1960s, and there were some inspiring models. Grotowski's Polish Laboratory Theatre in Wroclaw held up the tantalizing example of an officially supported group able to carry out experimental work on a permanent basis in a provincial city. Then there was the Living Theatre, which literally roamed the world, a defiant, often-outlawed collective. There had been the example earlier in the century of Jacques Copeau in France, who believed the individual should give way to the group and periodically took the members of his Théâtre du Vieux-Colombier off to the South for spells of outdoor physical labor.[9]

[9] See Sainer, I7-40;Cole & Chinoy, 218.

To a considerable extent this devotion to the idea of ensem- ble was in reaction to the prevailing traditions in the estab- lished theatre, with its star system, its auditions, and its com- mercialism--which in turn, of course, was but a reflection of a capitalistic, hierarchical society. The NST actors laid particular emphasis on being in a noncompetitive relation to each other. Joseph Chaikin emphasized this in his writing, as did Viola Spolin. Of course, the oral nature of their work *required* cooperation insofar as their method of creation was improvisa- tional. The actors learned from experience that good improvisation was impossible in a climate of mistrust and ego- centeredness. In fact, Richard Schechner was to assert that the actor's sense of group “determines aesthetic life."

A thorny element in the equation was the question of leadership. The political spirit of the 1960s demanded a democratic ethos, but in practice the NST theatre groups tended to coalesce around a particularly dynamic individual who put a strong stamp on it. What undoubtedly contributed to leadership problems was the absence of a positive role model.

The literary theatre was no example, with its authoritarian and often distinctly inhumane ways of treating actors, and prelite- rary examples, even to those few who might have known them, were too exotic. As an illustration, Richard Schechner describes with lingering pain the occasion in his career as founder-director of the Performance Group when he lost his collective spirit: “I substituted raw authority: I became boss.” Not unexpectedly, his company revolted, leaving Schechner, writing about the subject later, distinctly ambivalent: “The thoughts I have about groups are mixed.”[10]

[10] Chaikin, 59; Schechner 1973,280, 255, 265.

INSISTENCE ON INTERACTION

The NST groups showed a strong interest in eliminating the separation between actors and audience that is so hallowed a part of literary theatre tradition. The point was made in many ways. The Living Theatre actors mixed with the audience during intermission. Richard Schechner, who yearned for “village-like” responses from audiences, was known to ask the audience to remove their shoes prior to entering the theatre. In fact, actors from more than one group, the Performance Group included, invited audience members in the heat of performance to join them in taking off their clothes altogether. Peter Brook said he wanted a “natural” audience and a context in which “both sides, actors and audience, relax to the point where judgment and defenses melt into shared experience." Boal went even further in developing what he called “simultaneous dramaturgy," in which audience suggestions shaped the ending of the piece. Many community and therapeutically oriented theatres adopted a model in which actors presented a skit, then remained in role while the audience interviewed them. The modus vivendi of the satirical improv theatre was to utilize audience suggestions as provocations for the skits.

Perhaps no approach went so far as psychodrama, which even eschewed actors as a separate group: Moreno called for our own “sister and brother” and “friends and neighbors" to be actors, and individuals who came along to watch often found themselves either as protagonists in their own life dramas or actors in someone else's. What this approach lacked in polish it made up for in authenticity. It had a kind of compulsive appeal, which Eric Bentley recognized when he mused that he found himself travelling uptown to see Moreno's open session psychodramas rather than downtown to the productions of the avant-garde. In short, across the NST spectrum there was a common commitment to share the liberation of drama, to “open the doors of the granary,” as Julian Beck put it, to transform a passive experience on the part of the spectators into an active and interactive one.[11]

[11] See Heilpern, 89; Fox 1987, 59; Bentley, 386; Beck, 27.

Moreno had been practicing psychodrama publicly since the 1930s. The Living Theatre gained attention in the late fifties. Viola Spolin's widely read book on theatre games in education, *Improvisation for the Theater*, was published in 1963. The period of greatest activity in the protest and experimental theatre movement was the late sixties and early seventies, and the community-focussed therapeutic theatre companies emerged in the late seventies. It is beyond our scope to examine whether one school influenced another, but undoubtedly with such a commonality of values among the NST groups there was much cross-fertilization. To take three immediate examples: comic-satiric improv director Paul Sills, as I have mentioned, is Viola Spolin's son; Paul Binder, founder of the Big Apple Circus, belonged to the San Francisco Mime Troupe, and Maryat Lee, the leader of Ecotheatre, an Appalachian mountain group, was influenced by Moreno's psychodrama.[12]

[12] See French, 32. For a European example of cross-fertilization, see Feldhendler.

A NEW SENSE OF STAGE

A final common feature of the NST groups is their two- pronged rejection of the literary stage space. First there emerged a new interest in the outdoors as a venue for theatre.
Groups such as the Bread and Puppet Theatre made natural settings very much a part of their aesthetic, and the English group Welfare State developed a new kind of environmental theatre which focussed on creating transpersonal effects in outdoor space that were based on a concept of environmental attunement. Grotowski, for his part, shifted his attention from the indoor experimental theatre to paratheatrical experience outdoors. Peter Brook even led a troupe of actors across Africa, performing in fields and village maidans.

The second response was to simplify the indoor staging of the theatre. Grotowski wrote: 
    We found that theatre can exist without make-up, without autonomic costume and scenography, without a separate performance area (stage), without lighting and sound effects. It cannot exist without the actor- spectator relationship of perceptual, direct “live" com- munion. The Rich Theatre depends on an artistic kleptomania...Consequently, I propose poverty in the theatre."[13]

[13] Grotowski, I9.

Thus, in contrast to a literary theatre committed to artifice, the NST groups readily adapted themselves to the existing environment. A lawn was once again OK for theatre, and ordinary rooms were, too.

Where does Playback Theatre fit on the tree of new developments? Many people place it on the therapeutic theatre branch because I have an extensive background in Moreno's psychodrama, and there is a close connection between the Playback Theatre and psychodrama communities. At the same time, the genesis of Playback was also stimulated by my university study of oral epics, living in Nepal, and growing up as a keen follower of modern art, dance, and experimental theatre in New York. My own belief is that Playback Theatre, in its steadfast allegiance to orality, is somewhere mid-way up the trunk, capable of reaching into many of the branches.[14]

[14] For a comparison of Playback Theatre and psychodrama, see
Fox 1991.

In this chapter, we have discussed some of the ways in which the NST shows strong affinity with our preliterary drama para- digm--specifically, in the areas of preferring a theatre of action and the senses over a theatre of words; creating collectively; seeking an intimate involvement with audiences; and choosing to be “poor"--i.e, performing with a minimum of cost and technological sophistication.
