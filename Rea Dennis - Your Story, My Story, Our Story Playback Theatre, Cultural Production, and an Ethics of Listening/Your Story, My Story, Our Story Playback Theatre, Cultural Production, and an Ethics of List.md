# Your Story, My Story, Our Story: Playback Theatre, Cultural Production, and an Ethics of Listening

Rea Dennis

## Abstract

> Playback Theatre is a contemporary community-based performance form that is derivative of the oral tradition merging sacred ritual, personal story, and community gatherings. The centrality of audience members’ stories to the performance process creates opportunities for inclusive participatory arts practice. This paper explores the way in which storytelling opportunities that are generated through participation in the Playback Theatre event constitute sites of cultural production. The interweaving of narrative, folkloric, and community-based performance theory with examples from practice informs the discussion about the way in which the telling of and listening to personal narratives acts toward cultural production based on anti-oppressive values and an ethics of listening.
> 
> We pass our culture and lore on through talking, through talking and the stories we tell through our bark paintings, and our performance, our song and dance performance. A combination of all three together (Wanyubi Marika, 2003). ¹

I begin with a reference to pre-modern cultural life in recognition of the heritage within storytelling practice. When I first read these words by the great aboriginal elder of the Rirratjingu people of northeast Arnhem Land, I felt both grief and relief. Initially my grief was associated with my knowledge of the impact of the colonial invasion and the fragmentation of their cultural lives. The relief was linked to my knowledge of the emerging indigenous re-claiming of culture and land. Perhaps more enlivening is the way indigenous artists like Wesley Enoch, Deborah Mailman, and Leah Purcell, e.g., were claiming the structures of more Western theatrical language into which they imported traditional forms (Casey, 2005). It wasn’t until later that I identified another kind of grief, something to do with the loss or absence of a culture and lore tradition in my life. This is not an uncommon feeling for the children of migrants in contemporary Australia. The reclamation of indigenous rights has brought into sharp relief the injustices of the past and a questioning of what it means to be Australian and what rights one must invoke in order to claim this identification. There was also a secondary experience of relief in acknowledging that, in large part, my own performance practice delivered a sort of cultural dialectic with the indigenous culture and with the ever-changing landscape of Australian identity. The foundation of this practice combined cultural context with personal story and community-based performance. This essay examines the function of personal storytelling in the public domain as an avenue for cultural production. The interweaving of narrative, folkloric, and community-based performance theory with examples from my Playback Theatre practice enables a discussion on the way in which the telling of and the listening to personal stories acts toward cultural production and civic dialogue.

## PERSONAL STORIES AND CULTURAL PRODUCTION

> Each story spun into other stories, binding us together so that process became community, however brief the time (Daveson, 2001, p. 38).

Stories and storytelling feature across various social, political, and therapeutic practices. Bauman (1986) argues that the telling of stories and story-based practices are principal to community and cultural life. Rappaport (2000) concurs, claiming that this is premised on the belief that “community cannot be a community without a shared narrative” (p. 6). Salas claims that the intention of Playback Theatre is to “form a collective story about a community of people” by rendering the individual experience meaningful in a public arena where people “become part of a shared sense of purposeful existence” (Salas, 1993, p. 22). This idea of purposeful existence is linked to the telling of personal stories and is in direct opposition to the ideas contained in traditional stories that might in Zipes’ (2005) terms merely redistribute conservative values and ideas that repressively maintain the status quo. Zipes states, “Instead of gaining a sense of tradition through personal experience and experimentation, we are to learn, often by rote, what our traditions are and come to know ourselves through mass-mediated and manipulated stories” (2005, p. 7).

Inherent in Zipes’ (2005) words is the mandate to place local stories at the center of community processes. In this way there exists scope for the collective discovery of culture and experience, and of individual and collective agency in cultural production. Kershaw (1992), too, is critical of imposed narratives, claiming they are oppressive and cheat us of the right to make our own culture. In Kershaw’s opinion, by shifting from the prescriptive constraints of the democratization of culture toward the more liberating social practice of cultural democracy, citizens are enabled to actively oppose the exclusive dominant discourses. Perhaps in counterpoint to the more conservative act of storytelling as a transmission of culture, Playback Theatre privileges local and personal stories and so fosters sites of cultural production and the expression of an emerging culture and lore.

Playback Theatre is a community-based performance form in which personal stories are told in a public performance, where this telling is juxtaposed with improvised dramatizations informed by the told story. The impetus of the Playback Theatre methodology came from Jonathan Fox in upstate New York in the early 1970s (Dauber, 1999). The emergence of Playback Theatre in the mid-1970s is commensurate with a general trend that Alan Read (1993) links to the need to be integral to some collective purposeful effort. Grassroots activities became the stock standard response to the depersonalized, generalized, unified views of modernity and have facilitated somewhat of a revival of the pre-modern place and importance of story. The critique of the grand narratives and the move toward reclaiming the authority of local and personal story coincide in the Playback Theatre. Influenced by his own exploration of 20th century experiential theatre and a fascination with pre-literary storytelling and other oral cultural forms, Fox (1994) set out to develop a theatre form that espoused values of social justice and yet delivered on theatricality and mystery; a form that he would want to practice. Over the next few years the elements of the Playback Theatre method emerged in an experimental and collaborative process informed by the various influences of the ensemble that became known as The Original Company. Alongside the early theatrical experimentation, Fox was also influenced by the work of J. L. Moreno and his psychodrama method. Thus it could be claimed that this influence on the development and understanding of the playback method saw it find equal application as a form of therapeutic theatre and as community-based performance. During the 1980s and 90s, the practice of Playback Theatre spread to Australia and New Zealand and to countries in Europe, Asia, and South America, and it continued to develop in the United States. Since 2000, it has premiered in the African countries of Angola and Burundi. The utility of the method and its ease of translation to different social, political, and cultural contexts have brought with it an increased scrutiny and a series of questions about ethics, values, rights, and agency.

Spontaneous and improvised Playback Theatre occurs in a call-and-response type rhythm. Following traditional call and response structures, the call in playback is to tell a story, i.e., to recount an experience from one’s life. The response then emerges in the form of a voluntary audience participant who moves from the auditorium onto the stage to tell his or her story. Throughout the telling, the storyteller is supported by and at times prompted by the ritual shaman, or conductor, to select from a group of actors on stage as they cast their story. After the telling, the story is then dramatized spontaneously by the playback players who are citizen actors. ² The second presentation of the story is an aggregation of the players’ interpretations and could be considered the community response. In some way, this enactment calls forth the next story. A new teller then emerges from the audience group and recounts a different story that again acts as a response to the former improvized enactment, and so on. This amalgamation of personal storytelling and improvised performance facilitates participation in real cultural activities in the way Kershaw recommends. Why *personal* stories?

## STORYTELLING AND SOCIAL CHANGE: A POSTCOLONIAL PERSPECTIVE

Stories are imbued with the cultural, social, political, and historical truths and provide a bridge between our sense of self and our sense of other. Stories help us structure our world and make meaning of our lives. Meanwhile, storytelling is constitutive of our social life. Bauman (1986) claims that in the sharing of stories there “is a deep sense of context and of social action” (p. 113). This essay is concerned with stories and storytelling in the context of community-based performance. Bruner (1986) argues that enabling the intimate interactions embedded in the sharing of stories to occur in a public forum paves the way for societal and personal reforms. Telling a story is a way of redrawing maps and finding new destinations (Frank, 1995). And stories enable us to reflect. It is this that leads Razack (1993) to examine the value of storytelling for social change. She champions the revelations in personal stories as a means by which to contest established knowledge and expose those experiences of the world that are not admitted into dominant knowledge paradigms. In the Australian context it is useful to consider this alongside a post-colonial position in relation to the relevance of personal stories in social change. Frank (1995) suggests that “Post-colonialism in its most generalized form is the demand to speak rather than being spoken for and to represent oneself rather than being represented, or in the worst cases, rather than being effaced entirely” (p. 13).

This has particular significance in those contexts in which social distance and power differentials are at play. One example of this is in audiences in which there are disabled and non-disabled citizens. I draw on the following excerpt from an interview with Brett, ³ a man with a physical disability who also worked as an advocate in the disability sector. He is speaking about a Playback Theatre performance that occurred in the context of a National Week for Disability Action. He says:

> It’s the real stories, and meeting those people who tell them, that makes this a powerful experience. [Others can then see that] we are not just case studies, we are living emotional experiences. If the person themselves can tell their story, it’s going to have far more reaching effects than if a CEO or a therapist tell them for us! (Brett interview, 2002).

Brett makes a direct reference to his right to tell his own story, to tell his version of life in this community. He also discriminates between other possible versions of his story-a version that could be told by a CEO of a disability organization or a version that could be told be a therapist, thereby identifying that the access he has had to the community-based forum has been fundamental to his access to contributing to the cultural production within the event. Yet he goes on to emphasise that it is not just his telling of his story that is important but who else tells, and who is present to witness these tellings. He says:

> [The Playback Theatre performance] needed to have a variety of stories, about a variety of life experiences, from a variety of people. It needed more points of view: carer, consumer, professionals, [the] general community; the point of view of friends or maybe (of) the enemy. Even on the same topic, you can have different acting, different perspectives from different people. This would give insight into the way that other people think. For example, some health professionals are very stigmatizing and disempowering towards consumers, it could help if they saw different perspectives. [It should] include members of the general public-it’s about Disability Action (Brett interview, 2002).

Brett’s comments suggest that it is important to ensure a diversity of voices when calling forth stories in a group with diverse life experiences. In this way a kind of conversation-in-stories is possible. He is demanding an active performance of culture where people can experience their “values, belief system, hopes, fears, and aspirations through participating in an expressive act” (Cohen-Cruz, 2005, p. 12). Perhaps equally as valid is the potential for reciprocity in the telling and the witnessing of each other’s stories that is often lacking in relationships between people with and without disabilities. Brett acknowledges that contemporary story-based processes work to assist us to invest our own (and others’) experiential life with more significance and perhaps experience a change of perspective. This potential for dialogue is what appears to underpin the relevance of Playback Theatre in diverse cultural contexts. Dialogue appears plausible in the playback performance because it is equally a theatre of listening and it facilitates a kind of mutuality and co-existence.

The post-colonial critique of the resurgence in the telling of personal stories is seen as a way to counteract previous practices that might have contributed to silencing voices like those of tellers with a disability. Anti-oppressive and feminist critics claim that everyone has the right to speak their truth in their own words. Yet it is worth considering the implicit risk in facilitating live (improvised) storytelling; perhaps most likely is the possibility of there being a perceived pressure to tell. While Brett expresses a desire to tell in the right context, a perceived pressure to tell could be problematic and is intensified by the risk of exploitation within story-based processes. The issue of who owns stories and how individual stories may be consumed is topical. There is some debate with respect to the ethical practice. For example, Shuman (1986) likens the control over the use of stories told in everyday life to the copyright author’s claim over “manuscripts prepared for publication” (p. 1). However, this position is challenged when juxtaposed with Frank’s position of stories being a liberation of multiple voices. He writes, “In stories, the teller not only recovers her voice; she becomes a witness to the conditions that rob others of their voice. . . . When any person recovers his voice, many people begin to speak through that story” (Frank, 1995, p. xii).

This is a fraught area with a high potential for re-colonizing oppression. Salverson (1999) extends this in her analysis of storytelling as a site in which an “aesthetics of injury” drives the art practice, particularly when using personal storytelling in refugee contexts. Meanwhile Reinharz (1992) concedes that we need not silence ourselves to let others be heard. In fact, she urges that we use our authority to help bring other voices forward. Such is the reality in the Playback Theatre method, where there are various points at which performers collaborate with the teller to bring their voice forward. What is clear from the post-colonial perspective is that this must not undermine the agency of the storyteller, nor distort the purpose of the telling. It is the care taken to enact these values and the transparency that arises from the exchange of stories that renders the experience dialogic. This requires that personal story and listening meet at the center with an ethics of voice and a morality of listening guiding the process. The art practice then provides the aesthetic language and a means of celebration of collective culture and identity born of individual stories and creative expression.

## EXPERIENCE, STORYTELLING, AND REFLEXIVITY

Much of the discussion in narrative, life history, and folkloric writings about the form and content of stories revolves around the conceptualization of experience. Shuman (1986) claims that “stories categorize experience” (p. 20) and makes a distinction in the relationship between stories, experiences, and events. In Bruner’s (1986) writing, the distinction is between experience, reality, and expression. Building on the work of Bruner, Denzin (1989) suggests that:

> Persons as selves have experiences, experience referring here to the individuals meeting, confronting, passing through, and making sense of events in their lives… experiences may be problematic, routine, or ritual-like. Problematic experiences are also called epiphanies, or moments of revelation in a person’s life (Denzin, 1989, p. 33).

It is likely that the desire to tell a story is somehow fuelled by the original experience. Denzin distinguishes between self stories and personal experience narratives. He states that “a self story positions the self of the teller centrally in the narrative that is given. It is literally a story of and about self in relation to an experience” (Denzin, 1989, p. 43), whereas a personal experience narrative is a story someone tells “about their personal experience” (Stahl, in Denzin, 1989, p. 43). In an effort to elucidate how these two versions of a story differ, Denzin concludes that:

> Personal experience narratives are more likely to be anecdotal, everyday, commonplace experiences, while self stories involve pivotal, often critical, life experiences. Self stories need not be coherent, linear, accounts. They need not entertain or recreate cherished values and memories of a group, while personal experience narratives do (Stahl, 1977, p. 19). Self stories … are often mandated by the group, while personal experience narratives may be told only to another individual (Denzin, 1989, p. 44).

Bauman (1986) adds the dimension of point of view to the discussion when he reports that regardless of how we classify the story that is told, it is necessary to make the point that the term “personal experience” implies “a particular point of view.”

> That is, the event recounted in these narratives is purportedly one in which the person telling the story was originally personally involved, and the point of view from which the event is recounted is that of the narrator by virtue of his or her participation in that event (Bauman, 1986, p. 33-34).

This distinction is pertinent to the Playback Theatre method, where the space that opens up has conditions placed upon it, e.g., the audience participant who elects to tell a story, consents to tell a story from his or her own point of view, and that features that person. This implicates the teller in the act of telling in the playback performance and opens a reflexive space. Giroux (1992) writes that such spaces must embrace a shifting ground of “multiple and heterogeneous borders where different histories, languages, experiences, and voices intermingle amid diverse relations of power and privilege” (p. 32).

Implicit in Giroux’s plea for us to embrace the shifting ground is the way in which telling our stories enables reflection and dialogue. In this way storytelling processes are applicable to social change agendas. Kalantzis (2001) is similarly convinced and urges that we dare to tell the second story of our history, the story that is much harder to tell because “it is bound up with the problem of how to remember things that you don’t want to remember.” She claims that “to be true to ourselves, we must struggle to tell both stories as one” (Kalantzis, 2001, p. 20). Writing after the Balkan wars, Todorov (in Mantovani, 2000) admits that telling the harder stories contravenes how we are compelled to collectively remember. He posits that:

> Collective memory in general prefers to conserve two types of past situations: those in which we were victorious heroes, and those in which we were innocent victims. … The least glorious pages of our past would be the most instructive … not when it serves to nourish resentment but when its bitter taste drives us to transform ourselves (Todorov, in Mantovani, 2000, p. 119).

Yet, the act of juxtaposing stories from the center with stories from the margins in the way that Kalantzis urges nurtures the possibility of reciprocity when Australians meet each other. The reflexive opportunity afforded through storytelling enables us to “turn around on the past and alter the present in its light, or alter the past in the light of the present” (Bruner, in Frank, 1995, p. 65). Frank develops this thesis further drawing on Kierkegaard among others to suggest that the act of telling our own stories implicates a certain kind of ethical responsibility in the tellers for their lived lives. This construction of the storytelling acts as ethical enables us to face up to “positions that might challenge and interrogate [our] own location” (Conquergood, in Mienczakowski, 1997, p. 167). This can invite self-reflexivity, critical empathy, and a capacity to listen across differences. This position is well represented by Darcy, who attended a Playback Theatre performance in which audience participants elaborated stories of intercultural meetings and global mobility under the guise of “travel stories.”

Darcy ⁴ comes onstage and begins to recount an experience she had some 15 years earlier when she was traveling in Turkey. “We (my girlfriend and I) were hitchhiking and a car pulled over and offered us a lift. We immediately knew that we were doing something very risky and I experienced incredible fear when I realized it was three Muslim men who speak no English that were in the car.” In her telling she explains that they accepted the ride for fear of drawing attention to themselves! She describes herself crouching in the back of the car where she and her girlfriend concoct an escape plan that includes “abandoning the luggage and running” if there is a chance. They are astounded when the men pull over and excitedly gesture to them. After cautious moments they emerge from the car and find that their fear is transformed into bewilderment, then embarrassment, as they realize that the men are urging them to witness the awesome sight in a herd of deer grazing by the roadside near sunset. While Darcy told her story in a spontaneous and unself-conscious way, she describes her experience of watching the improvised enactment of her story as “humbling.” She says:

> The kind of honesty that it invites is interesting. When I told my story, to actually have to admit to feeling the fear in seeing the black men was a thing where I felt ashamed, if I think about it. But that was there, that was me, that was what I was like. So that comes up in the story. I wouldn’t feel like that now, but I did then. Looking at that and experiencing the kind of shame that comes from admitting that. I thought, “I wonder if they all think I’m a racist” (Darcy, interview, 2002).

This excerpt shows Darcy’s reflection on her younger self. She tells the second story that Kalantzis is calling for. This “ethics of recollection” has seen her share her memories of past actions, and in doing so she is showing these actions to others and in this way taking some responsibility for them (Frank, 1995, p. 132). Darcy’s story reveals risky themes of racism and xenophobia. Giroux (1992) advocates that spaces on the “borders” such as those evoked in a ritual performance like Playback Theatre enable a new kind of resistance. That is, a space where we might encounter ourselves and The Other outside the protocols of everyday life, and in this encounter, see ourselves and our interactions between and with The Other in a way that is removed and with the potential for insight as shown in Darcy’s experience. Mienczakowski (1997) commends the efficacy of reflexivity and an outward-looking orientation during this time in which there is a “preoccupation” with self (p. 163). Boje (1991) identifies another level, a meta-level, of reflexivity that is activated when a teller then begins to tell stories about his or her experience of telling stories. The truth of stories is not simply what was experienced but equally what is experienced in the telling of the story and in its reception (Frank, 1995).

A dialogic space is “critically responsive to its context” (p. 330). Drawing from his community-based theatre practice in South Africa, Rule (2004) suggests that to be dialogic in the Freirian sense, the activity must be “socially situated” and linked to a “transformative agenda” (p. 324). Playback Theatre is such a space where individuals and groups draw on their own experiences to participate in a dialogic exploration of culture. It is the reciprocity in the Playback Theatre event that gives momentum to the dialogic dynamic. Listeners engage in a process of unpacking “the relationship between the incidents described and the interpretation of what actually happened” (Shuman, 1986, p. 21). In listening to one another, there is the potential for moments of mutual witness suggesting that such attention to the other renders this listening a moral act. Razack (1993) claims that it is the act of listening to the story that embodies the liberatory potential of storytelling. Indeed, listening to another person’s story can orient one a counter point of view (Cohen-Cruz, 2005). In listening we hear beyond dominant hegemonic discourses to the stories that embody what might be one of the *second* stories. Thus, to enact dialogue the challenge is in the reception: the hearing, the seeing, the feeling, the emergent response. In this dialectic there is a third space-the borderlands. At these borders, participants’ differences are amplified as we look for a place to meet each other, for a connection, a bridge. The interplay between teller, players, and audience; among listening and watching; between interpreting and responding are all sites for the meetings that are possible on the borders in the Playback Theatre performance. The communicative channels enabled in the theatre event are by their nature relational and help to highlight differences between human beings. In this way, the dialogic experience is a way of bridging across differences. Burkett (2003) suggests such approaches must be favored if we are to move toward experiences of community that embrace intercultural meetings. While this sounds all well and good and has the essential values of empowerment and inclusion, it does not automatically serve that Playback Theatre is an unambiguous and uncomplicated site for the production of culture. Perhaps it is useful to turn now to the questions: who gets to tell the stories; and who gets to listen?

## ETHICS OF LISTENING

Rappaport (1995) argues that much of the work of social change and community development is in the direction of greater personal and collective empowerment. Perhaps a simple answer to who will tell is to turn to those who have been consistently silenced (Mienczakowski, 1997). Yet it does not automatically serve that any listener has a right to demand those voices to speak. While stories may break silences, they may also encourage listeners to view sociopolitical problems in individualistic terms. Stories tend to compel our attention. Razack (1993) reminds that there exists in most of us a deep and abiding curiosity to have that rare glimpse into the complex diversity and rich texture of the lives of others. This then points to the need for awareness about people’s similar right to remain silence. Such constraints are crucial in contexts supporting refugee and asylum seekers. ⁵ (Salverson, 1999).

There is also scope for inequity in projects driven by the desire to right past wrongs with regard to diversity and inclusive practice. Haedicke and Nellhaus (2001) suggest that it is this intention that makes community-based storytelling and performance political in nature, raising questions about social location, control, authority, and authenticity. They ask, “Who performs, whose material is performed, and who decides?” (p. 13). Rappaport (1995) is more direct, saying that if narratives are resources we should be able to see who is controlling them and who gives them social value otherwise there is a risk of social control, oppression, and disenfranchisement. He says:

> Everyone has stories, but some stories actively devalue people and other stories are not recognised as valuable at all. Some stories empower people and other stories disempower people (Rappaport, 1995, p. 3).

Cohen-Cruz (2005) and Haedicke and Nellhaus (2001) similarly argue for processes that are consciously geared toward empowerment over colonization. Such concerns are highly relevant to the critique of Playback Theatre’s application, where authorship and entitlement are contentious. Through the course of this discussion Playback Theatre has been explored as a culturally accessible forum through which to express and discuss various perspectives within a community-based performance event. In facilitating individuals and groups to draw on their own experiences in the production of culture, they can be supported to participate in a conversation-in-stories where multiple perspectives might be expressed in an inclusive forum. Perhaps this potential in a Playback Theatre performance illuminates the meaning of our existence and fosters links with one another and links with our past in the way Wanyubi Marika (2003) announces at the top of the paper. At the very least, the spaces on the borders evoked in the playback performance enable a kind of resistance, a space for reflection and a collaborative production of culture.
